How to Lead Any Meeting

The skills behind all three levels, whether it's your first meeting or your fiftieth.

1

Set the tone

Ground Rules

Many facilitators find that laying out a few ground rules at the start of each meeting, and enforcing them gently but firmly, helps the group run smoothly for everyone. For example:

  • Stoicism is a cosmopolitan philosophy, so the group is open to anyone.
  • We're here to explore Stoicism. Stay focused, and avoid drifting into general philosophical or political debates.
  • Use the meeting to practice the four cardinal virtues: wisdom (navigate a difficult discussion wisely), courage (speak up and contribute), justice (treat others fairly and with respect), and temperance (don't speak too little or too much).
  • If you've spoken a lot, pause before chipping in again so others have a chance.
  • The only things not tolerated are intolerance and rudeness, which may lead to temporary or permanent removal from the group.
2

Structure

Structure the Meeting

Most stoas meet monthly for about 90 minutes. This format works for any topic, and every meeting guide follows it:

  1. A week before, announce: post the date, the question, and the reading on your Substack. See the announcement template.
  2. Open (10 minutes): welcome everyone, share your ground rules, and go around the room with names.
  3. Read (10 minutes): read a short passage aloud together.
  4. Discuss (45 minutes): work through a few questions. You don't need to finish them all.
  5. Practice (15 minutes): introduce a Stoic exercise and try it briefly together.
  6. Close (10 minutes): one takeaway from each person, then confirm the next date.
  7. A few days after, recap: post a short summary for members who missed it.

Prefer a different shape? Topic Ideas has other formats, from book clubs to case studies.

3

Lead

Lead the Discussion

  • Be the group's facilitator, not its lecturer. Your job is to draw others into the conversation.
  • Don't let any one person dominate the discussion. Be polite but firm.
  • If the conversation wanders too far from the topic, gently nudge it back.
  • If someone asks a question you can't answer, say so, as Socrates would. Ask whether anyone else knows, or note it to research and revisit next time.

The Socratic method

The Socratic method, or elenchus (cross-examination), is one of the best ways to run a fruitful, enjoyable discussion in a small group. Rather than making long statements, the facilitator asks probing questions, perhaps mixed with short explanations. A thesis (for example, from the reading) is examined, and alternatives are proposed and examined in turn. Often the result is aporia, a state of uncertainty that the ancients considered the beginning of wisdom.

  • Briefly explain how the method works, so participants know what to expect.
  • After you ask a question, be comfortable with silence. People need time to think and to work up the courage to speak.
  • Expect some “productive discomfort”: the method may lead people to question their own beliefs.
  • Welcome unusual ideas; discourage ones that aren't serious or that pull the group off topic.
  • Short clarifications are fine. Lectures are not.

Five moves to practice

Keep these questions handy. Each meeting guide suggests one.

  1. Clarify: “What do you mean by…?” Pin down words like good or fair before the group argues about them.
  2. Example: “Can you give an example?” Move from the abstract to lived experience.
  3. Evidence: “How would you know?” Test a claim without challenging the person.
  4. Test: “Is that always true?” Invite the group to find a counterexample.
  5. Consequence: “If that's true, what follows?” Follow an idea to where it leads.

And the Stoic one: “Is that in your control?”

4

Share

Share the Load

A stoa run by one person tends to end when that person's life gets busy. A co-facilitator covers absences, shares the prep, and keeps the group going if you step back. It's worth having at every stage, not just in your first year.

  • Look for the regular who comes back and helps without being asked.
  • Ask one person directly, for something specific, with an end date.
  • Share the role gradually: co-host, lead a segment, then run a meeting.

5

Sustain

Keep It Going

  • Share the planning, but lead. Most people want to participate, not organize. Ask members for topic ideas, but use your judgment about what will work.
  • Keep it interesting for you, too. Facilitators burn out less when some topics reflect their own interests.
  • Vary topics and voices. Rotate topics and presenters, and invite an outside speaker from time to time, such as a philosophy or history faculty member from a local college.
  • Announce every meeting. Post each meeting on your Substack page (or event service), then post a short recap afterward. Get the Word Out has templates and more ways to find new members.
  • Make room to socialize. If you can, follow the meeting with an informal social. Connection is a big part of why people come.
  • Don't take attendance personally. Some people become regulars; others show up now and then. That's the nature of the thing.
