Your Roadmap to Starting a Stoa

The Stoic Fellowship doesn't dictate how you run your group. Beyond a few basics for member stoas, you're free to build the community that best serves the people around you. These four steps take you from first idea to your first meeting. From there, Run a Stoa takes over.

1

Strategy

Decide What Kind of Group You'll Run

  • Where: in person, virtual, or a hybrid of both.
  • How often: we suggest meeting at least monthly, ideally on the same day, time, and place (for example, the third Sunday of each month at 4pm).
  • How big: discussion works best with about 8–12 people. Larger groups may want to break into smaller circles. It's okay if your first meetings are smaller; it takes time to grow.
  • How much guidance you'd like: most new stoas follow The Guided Year, which plans every meeting. You can also pick from our Meeting Guides in any order, or plan your own with Topic Ideas.
  • Who could help you: a stoa is easier to run with two. You don't need a co-facilitator to start, but aim to have one by your sixth month. A friend, or a regular from your first meetings, is a good place to look. See Find Your Co-Facilitator.
2

Logistics

Find a Place to Meet

Look for somewhere quiet and easy to reach. Noisy places don't work, and restaurants aren't great either, since you'll have to talk between food orders. Places that work for many communities:

  • Outdoors (hiking or sitting, weather permitting)
  • Indoor public atria
  • A reserved room in a library, university, or community center
  • Quiet coffee shops, pubs, or bars without table service
  • Members' homes (not recommended for new stoas)

Meeting virtually? Choose a video platform your members can join easily, and use the same link each time.

3

Reach

Get the Word Out

Before your first meeting, let people know your stoa exists. We recommend you pick at least two channels:

  • Register your stoa on our map. It takes about five minutes, and we'll connect you with people nearby who are looking for a group.
  • Post flyers where people go to improve themselves: libraries, gyms, bookstores, community centers.
  • Start a free Substack page to announce each meeting and keep in touch between them.
  • Word of mouth: invite friends, and ask early members to bring somebody.
  • Online event services (optional) such as Meetup, Luma, or Eventbrite, where people in your area already look for local groups.

4

Preparation

Prepare Your First Meeting

Use the weeks before your first meeting to get ready. You don't need to have all the answers, but you should be comfortable talking about the basics. If someone asks something you can't answer, it's fine to say so, as Socrates did, and look into it together.

  • Read your first meeting guide. If you're following The Guided Year, that's Session 1, What Is Stoicism? Do the reading and try the discussion questions yourself.
  • Brush up on the basics with Stoicism 101, a brief outline of the philosophy's core ideas.
  • Go deeper with our study resources: books, ancient texts, videos, and courses.
