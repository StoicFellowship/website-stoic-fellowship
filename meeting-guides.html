What’s Actually Good?

If money, health, and reputation aren’t the goal, what is?

Reading · three pages

Plato, Euthydemus 278e–282e. Socrates argues that wealth and health only benefit us when they’re used wisely, so wisdom is what makes anything good.

Before the meeting

  1. Name three things you want most. Why do you want them?
  2. Can wealth or health ever harm someone? How?
  3. Socrates says wisdom is what makes other things useful. Do you agree?
  4. The Stoics called money and health “preferred indifferents.” What’s gained and lost in that view?
  5. Looking back on your meetings so far, what’s one idea you’ve actually used?

The wise-person test. Before a decision, ask: what would a wise person do here, and why?

What’s in My Control?

Why do we spend so much energy on things we can’t change?

Reading · one page

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1. Some things are up to us: our judgments, intentions, and actions. Others aren’t: our bodies, reputations, and other people.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack a week ahead with the question above and a link to the reading.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Try it on a real worry
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. List what worried you this week. Which items were fully up to you?
  2. Is your health in your control? Your job? Where exactly is the line?
  3. If outcomes aren’t up to us, why try at all?
  4. What changes when you aim at your effort rather than the result?
  5. Where could this idea go wrong if it’s taken too far?

Practice this month

Mine / not mine. Once a day, split a worry into the part that’s yours and the part that isn’t. Act only on yours.

Facilitator’s move

“How would you know?” When someone says something is out of their control, ask how they’d tell.

When Your Mind Jumps to Conclusions

What am I being asked to believe—and what supports it?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, especially its closing instruction; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.49. Epictetus asks us to examine impressions and what is up to us. Marcus separates what is reported from what we add. An impression is how something appears; assent accepts what it presents as true.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack a week ahead with the question and reading links. Invite an ordinary example of a quick conclusion. Newcomers need no preparation.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Aloud, together
Practice · 20Test a message with no reply
Discuss · 40The questions below
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. An important message has no reply. What do you know, and what might your mind add?
  2. Which impressions feel most convincing before you examine them? Why?
  3. What judgment or intention belongs to you, and what depends on someone else?
  4. What evidence would justify accepting, rejecting, or leaving a conclusion unsettled?
  5. If your first judgment stands after examination, what did the practice accomplish?

Practice this month

Test one impression. When certainty or urgency arrives, name the impression, separate what you know from what you infer, and decide what the grounds support. The judgment may stand, change, or stay unsettled. If you notice only afterward, you can still reopen it.

Facilitator’s move

“What supports that?” Ask it of reassuring interpretations too. The Fundamental Rule identifies your judgment and intention; another person’s response still deserves care.

Anger

Is it the thing that happened, or what I tell myself about it?

Reading · two short passages

Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: we aren’t disturbed by things, but by our judgments about them. Seneca, On Anger 2.4 (Book II, chapter 4): anger comes in three movements: an involuntary jolt, a judgment we agree to, and a passion we can’t control. We can step in at the second.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Ask people to notice one moment of irritation during the week.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Rewrite one judgment
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Recall a recent moment of anger. What happened, and what did you tell yourself about it?
  2. Seneca says the first jolt isn’t up to us, but agreeing to it is. In your example, where could you have stepped in?
  3. Seneca thought anger is never useful. Do you agree?
  4. What’s the difference between anger and a firm, fair response?
  5. What would a pause change: counting, walking away, sleeping on it?

Practice this month

Catch the second movement. When you feel the jolt, finish the sentence “I’m telling myself that…” before you act. Then ask whether it’s true.

Facilitator’s move

“Can you give an example?” Talk about anger stays vague until someone describes a real moment.

When a Difficulty Becomes a Disaster

What is the difficulty—and what am I predicting it means?

Reading

Seneca, Letters 13.4–⁠12; Epictetus, Enchiridion 4. Seneca examines anticipated troubles and the evidence for them. Epictetus prepares for familiar obstacles while preserving sound choice. Preparation acknowledges a possibility; it does not establish that the feared event will happen.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the question and reading links. Invite an ordinary worry about a task or conversation. Personal crises need not become discussion examples.

Open · 10Welcome; passing is fine
Read · 10Selected passages aloud
Practice · 20A difficult presentation: facts vs. predictions
Discuss · 40The questions below
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Before a difficult presentation, which problems are known and which are predictions?
  2. How do discomfort, uncertainty, or embarrassment affect the confidence you give a judgment?
  3. What evidence would support the feared conclusion—and what would weaken it?
  4. How does rehearsing one realistic obstacle differ from repeatedly imagining disaster?
  5. If the difficulty is real, what remains yours to judge without minimizing the risk?

Practice this month

Name the difficulty precisely. In one pressured situation, state the present facts, name the prediction or evaluation added, and decide what the evidence supports. You may also rehearse one likely obstacle and a sound response. Leave unsupported predictions open.

Facilitator’s move

“What is happening now?” Separate current evidence from a future story. A more cheerful forecast needs evidence too; reasonable precautions can proceed under uncertainty.

Before You Judge Someone

What did this person do—and what have I added?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 45 and 43. The first passage separates description from judgments that need further knowledge. The second acknowledges a brother’s injustice while recalling their relationship. A fuller perspective must be true and relevant; it need not excuse misconduct or restore trust.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the question and reading links. Ask for anonymous examples. Members may use the fictional case instead of a personal conflict.

Open · 10Ground rules; no identifying details
Read · 10Aloud, together
Practice · 20A colleague misses a deadline
Discuss · 40The questions below
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. A colleague misses an agreed deadline. What is established, and what remains uncertain?
  2. When does a judgment about conduct become a verdict about the whole person?
  3. What other true and relevant perspective would help you understand this conflict fairly?
  4. Can a justified grievance acquire an additional verdict that payback would be good?
  5. What remains owed to a fellow human being when an adverse judgment is justified?

Practice this month

Separate conduct from verdict. When judging someone, describe the conduct, name the further conclusion, and examine its grounds. Recall a relevant relationship or shared humanity without inventing a reassuring motive. Keep warranted criticism; revise what exceeds the evidence.

Facilitator’s move

“Which judgment are we examining?” Distinguish misconduct, motive, personal worth, and payback. Understanding a person does not settle what response justice requires.

When Wanting Turns Into Needing

Can I pursue this seriously without making a good life depend on getting it?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 2 and 4. Desire and aversion promise getting or escaping something. At the baths, Epictetus’s governing aim is to preserve sound choice. Preferred indifferents such as health and security deserve care; virtue determines whether we use them well.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the question and reading links. Invite members to notice a preference that feels like a requirement. A general example is enough.

Open · 10Optional practice check-in
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Preference or requirement?
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. What does something you want promise to give you—or protect you from?
  2. How can you tell when a preference becomes something that must happen?
  3. If the external goal stays the same, what changes when character governs the pursuit?
  4. Can an unwanted outcome bring a real loss without making living well impossible?
  5. Once the facts are settled, what would willingness to meet them mean without approving them?

Practice this month

Prefer without requiring. Choose one recurring want. Name its practical value, the condition you have attached to living well, and what stays worth wanting either way. When the demand returns, ask: “Am I preferring this, or requiring it?”

Facilitator’s move

“Necessary for what?” Separate an actual practical need from a claim about the possibility of a good life. Allow disappointment and difficult answers.

How Much Is Enough?

What would satisfy the need—and why do I still want more?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 15; Seneca, Letters 23.1–⁠8. Epictetus’s banquet image trains moderation in receiving and waiting for externals. Seneca directs joy toward sound purpose and conduct. We examine dependence on more and what we are learning to value.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Invite members to notice an expanding “enough”: possessions, praise, convenience, or certainty. Nobody needs to discuss finances.

Open · 10Optional practice check-in
Read · 10Selected passages aloud
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Define enough in one area
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. In one area of life, what would enough provide? What keeps moving the threshold?
  2. At the banquet, what changes when you receive your share without treating the table as yours?
  3. How could simplifying one convenience reveal whether you depend on it?
  4. What gladness comes from sound judgment or goodwill rather than getting another external reward?
  5. How can you appreciate ordinary advantages without making them conditions of living well?

Practice this month

Name enough. Choose one recurring want and state what would reasonably serve its purpose. When the threshold expands, examine what “more” promises; simplifying one small area can test the dependence. Notice what remains worth welcoming; no cheerful mood is required.

Facilitator’s move

“Enough for what purpose?” Needs differ with circumstances and responsibilities. Examine the reason for the threshold rather than ranking people by how little they own.

Before You Give In

What is this pleasure promising—and do I have grounds to agree?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 34 and Discourses 4.12.1–⁠5. Epictetus advises delay and considering pleasure’s aftermath. His account of attention also includes play and song. We apply these ideas to examining a compelling promise; enjoyment can still be a reasonable choice.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the reading links. Invite an ordinary example: scrolling, shopping, or another episode. No private habit needs to be disclosed.

Open · 10Welcome; no confessions required
Read · 10Aloud, together
Practice · 20Test the promise of one more episode
Discuss · 40The questions below
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. When an urge says “one more,” what exactly is it asking you to believe?
  2. What would a brief delay let you examine that the urge leaves out?
  3. How can you consider the aftermath without assuming all enjoyment leads to regret?
  4. What captures your attention repeatedly, and how does that shape what seems worth choosing?
  5. When might a limit chosen in advance help judgment—and when should you reconsider it?

Practice this month

Examine the promise. Pause in one recurring temptation, name the promised benefit, and consider the choice now and afterward. Accept, revise, or decline on grounds you can explain; an advance limit can help you remember them. Pleasure alone does not settle it.

Facilitator’s move

“What is the claim?” Find the judgment inside “I need this now.” Passing on pleasure is not automatically progress; more careful examination is.

The Stoic Practice · August What Am I Trying Never to Face? What am I treating as something that must never happen?

What Am I Trying Never to Face?

What am I treating as something that must never happen?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 2; Seneca, Letters 18.5–⁠8. Epictetus redirects aversion toward misusing our own choice. Seneca rehearses simple living to examine dependence on comfort and wealth. This modern adaptation uses a modest, safe inconvenience to inspect what we think we need.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Say the topic includes fear, comfort, and dependence. Nobody must disclose a loss or undertake discomfort. Bring the reading links.

Open · 10Welcome; anyone may pass
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Test a small supposed necessity
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. What inconvenience do you work hard to prevent? What would facing it seem to mean?
  2. When does sensible avoidance become a demand for complete protection from difficulty?
  3. What might going briefly without a convenience reveal that thinking alone misses?
  4. How can knowing life is finite clarify what is worth wanting without making us gloomy?
  5. In difficulty, what misuse of your own choice deserves more concern than discomfort?

Practice this month

Examine one aversion. Name what you want to avoid and why escape seems necessary. If suitable, try one harmless inconvenience, such as setting aside entertainment while waiting, and notice what it reveals about dependence. A recent unavoidable one works as well; no endurance contest is needed.

Facilitator’s move

“What are we testing?” Keep the focus on valuation. Choose ordinary discomfort that fits health and responsibilities; there is no need for deprivation or proof of toughness.

How do we love what we know we will lose?

Epictetus, Enchiridion 3 and 11. Remember that what you love is mortal. And of anything you lose, never say “I have lost it,” but “I have restored it.”

Knowing What to Do—and Doing It

What does this situation call for from me?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 30; Musonius Rufus, Lecture 5, “Which Is More Effective, Theory or Practice?” Relationships help identify responsibilities; Musonius puts practice ahead of explanation. An appropriate action is one you can reasonably justify. If obstructed, a different response may fit.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Ask members to notice one responsibility they understand but find hard to carry out. Nobody needs a journal or prior lessons.

Open · 10Optional practice check-in
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Give a virtue an action
Close · 10One practice to retain

Discussion questions

  1. In an ordinary responsibility, what makes an action fitting rather than merely convenient?
  2. How does being a friend, colleague, parent, or neighbor clarify your part?
  3. When someone else fails in their role, what changes about yours?
  4. If your plan is blocked, what concrete action could express the virtue now required?
  5. Looking across judgment, desire, and action, what small practice is worth retaining?

Practice this month

Give it an action. Choose one recurring responsibility. Identify what matters, decide “I will ___ because ___,” and take the next step. Repeat when it recurs; adapt when circumstances change. Assess your reasons and follow-through, not others’ cooperation.

Facilitator’s move

“What would that look like?” Turn “be patient” or “be responsible” into conduct someone could describe. A useful answer may include a refusal or boundary.

Making Room for Other People

Whose good is missing from what I want?

Reading

Seneca, Letters 48.1–⁠3; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6. Seneca links friendship with shared interests. Marcus separates helping from keeping account of what others owe. We examine whom our desires include and what return we require.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Invite members to notice a shared decision where their preference affects someone else. Include a reading link; preparation is optional.

Open · 10Optional practice check-in
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Widen a meeting-time preference
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. In a shared choice, whose interests do you include—and whose are easy to overlook?
  2. What would you hope for an overlooked person if someone close to you were in their place?
  3. How could taking those interests seriously change what you want, even if your preference remains?
  4. When you contribute, what recognition are you waiting for before it feels worthwhile?
  5. If someone declines your help, can their welfare remain important without their compliance?

Practice this month

Give the want a wider aim. Before one shared choice, name your preference and an overlooked legitimate interest, then finish: “I want ___ while taking ___ seriously.” Keep that concern without praise or agreement. No extra good deed is required.

Facilitator’s move

“Who else is affected?” Include someone who bears a cost without being present. Fair concern includes your own legitimate needs; it does not guarantee a compromise.

What contribution is mine to make?

A Practice You Can Keep

What deserves a lasting place in the way I live?

Reading

Seneca, Letters 20.1–⁠5; Epictetus, Discourses 3.2.1–⁠5. Seneca seeks agreement between principles and conduct. Epictetus distinguishes training in desire, action, and assent. Use these fields to find a practice that helps in a real recurring situation; keep the commitment sustainable.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Invite one remembered practice or question worth retaining. Notes are optional; newcomers may choose something useful from tonight’s discussion.

Close · 10One contribution each

Discussion questions

  1. Which practice helped you notice or do something differently in ordinary life?
  2. What difficulty remains, and which field—judgment, desire, or action—needs attention?
  3. When two legitimate responsibilities compete, how can one ethical standard guide different responses?
  4. What would make your chosen practice realistic on a crowded day?

Practice this month
