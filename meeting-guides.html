What Is Stoicism?

What would it mean to live well, whatever happens to you?

Reading

Stoicism 101. Give a short talk using Stoicism 101, or watch the five-minute video together.

Discussion questions

  1. What makes a good life, and how much does it depend on our circumstances?
  2. Pick an everyday frustration. What’s outside your control? What choices remain?
  3. A colleague takes credit for your work. What response would show these virtues?
  4. An important message goes unanswered. What do you know? What are you adding?

Pick One Practice: Morning intention · A deliberate pause · Evening review. Choose one, decide when you’ll use it, and try it this week.

Facilitator’s move

“What do you mean by…?” When someone says good, happy, or stoic, ask what they mean before going on.

What’s Actually Good?

If money, health, and reputation aren’t the goal, what is?

Reading

Plato, Euthydemus 278e–282e. Socrates argues that wealth, health, and beauty benefit us only when they are used wisely; in foolish hands they do harm. So wisdom is what makes anything good.

  2. Socrates says wisdom makes other things useful. Do you agree?
  3. Why call money and health “preferred indifferents”?
  4. If wisdom is the real good, what would you do differently?
  5. What’s one idea from our meetings you’ve actually used?

When the trouble starts with how you read a situation: what’s in your control, jumping to conclusions, anger, worry, and judging other people.

Reading · one page What’s in My Control? Why do we spend so much energy on things we can’t change?
Seeing Clearly About 90 minutes

What’s in My Control?

Why do we spend so much energy on things we can’t change?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1. Some things are up to us: our judgments, impulses, desires, and aversions. Others aren’t: our bodies, property, reputation, and position.

Before the meeting

Announce through your group’s usual channel a week ahead with the question above. Newcomers need no preparation.

Open · 10Welcome; one worry
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45Questions 1–5
Practice · 15Divide a real worry
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Think of that worry. Which parts of it were genuinely up to you?
  2. Is your health up to you? Your job? What part of each belongs to your choice?
  3. If outcomes aren’t up to us, why try at all?
  4. What changes when you aim at your effort rather than the result?
  5. Where could this idea go wrong if it’s taken too far?

Practice this month

Mine / Not Mine: Name the parts · Divide · Act on mine. When a worry pulls at you, ask: “What is genuinely mine here?”

Facilitator’s move

“How would you know?” When someone says something is out of their control, ask how they would tell.

Anger

Is it the thing that happened, or what I tell myself about it?

Reading

Seneca, On Anger 2.4; Epictetus, Enchiridion 5. Seneca: anger comes in three movements: an involuntary jolt, a judgment we agree to, and a passion that runs away with us. Epictetus (Enchiridion 5): we are disturbed by our judgments about things.

Discussion questions

  1. What happened, and what did you tell yourself about it?
  2. After the first jolt, where could you have stepped in?
  3. Seneca thought anger is never useful. Do you agree?
  4. What’s the difference between anger and a firm, fair response?
  5. What would a pause change: counting, walking away, waiting?

Practice this month

Catch the Second Movement: Feel the jolt · Finish the sentence · Ask if it’s true. When you feel the jolt, finish the sentence before you act.

Facilitator’s move

“Can you give an example?” Talk about anger stays vague until someone describes a real moment.

When Wanting Turns Into Needing

Can I want this without making living well depend on getting it?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 4. Epictetus prepares to bathe while preserving a sound use of choice through whatever disturbances the visit brings.

Difficult People

How do I stay decent toward people who make it hard?

Reading

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1. Marcus expects to meet meddling, ungrateful, and arrogant people. They act from ignorance of good and bad; they share his nature, and we are made to work together.

Discussion questions

  2. Think of that person. What might they believe that makes their behavior make sense?
  3. Does understanding someone mean excusing them?
  4. In a difficult relationship, what is genuinely up to you?
  5. What does justice ask of you toward people you don’t like?

Practice this month

Morning Preparation: Prepare · Understand · Choose. Before a hard encounter, say your own version of 2.1.

Facilitator’s move

“Is that always true?” When someone says “people like that never change,” invite a counterexample.

Examine Your Impressions

Is it true, or does it just feel true?

Reading

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (the closing lines). Meet every harsh impression by saying, “You are an impression, and not at all what you appear to be.” Then test it.
