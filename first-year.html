The First Year

A month-by-month plan for a new stoa that meets once a month. Each meeting is ready to run: a question, a short reading, discussion questions, and a practice for members to try before next time. Follow it in order, and adapt anything that doesn't suit your group.

Part of Run a Stoa. Prefer to shape your own meetings? See Topics and Formats.

The first six months start from everyday problems, so newcomers can join any month without catching up. The second six go deeper into the readings and into daily practice.

Every Month How Each Meeting Runs

  • A week before: announce the meeting on your Substack with the month's question and reading. See the announcement template.
  • Open (10 minutes): welcome everyone, share your ground rules, and go around the room with names.
  • Read (10 minutes): read the passage aloud together. Most are a page or less.
  • Discuss (45 minutes): work through the questions. You don't need to finish them all.
  • Practice (15 minutes): introduce the month's practice and try it briefly together.
  • Close (10 minutes): one takeaway from each person, then confirm the next date.
  • A few days after: post a short recap on your Substack.

Each month also suggests a facilitator's move: one Socratic question for you to practice while leading the discussion. See the Socratic method for all five.

What Is Stoicism?

What would it mean to live well, whatever happens to you?

Reading · 10 minutes

Stoicism 101. Give a short talk in your own words, drawing on Stoicism 101, or watch this five-minute video together.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack a week ahead with the question above. Newcomers need no preparation.

Open · 15Ground rules; names and what brought you
Read · 10Your short talk or the video
Discuss · 40The questions below
Practice · 10Introduce it
Close · 15Agree on a regular day and time

Discussion questions

  1. Before tonight, what did you think “stoic” meant? What surprised you?
  2. The Stoics said a good life depends on character, not circumstances. Do you believe that?
  3. Think of someone you admire for how they handle hardship. What do they actually do?
  4. Wisdom, courage, justice, temperance: which comes easiest to you? Which is hardest?
  5. What would make a group like this worth your time each month?

Practice this month

Evening review. Each night, ask: What went well? What didn’t? What will I do differently tomorrow?

Facilitator’s move

“What do you mean by…?” When someone says good or happy, ask them to define it first.

Month 2 of 12 About 90 minutes

What’s in My Control?

Why do we spend so much energy on things we can’t change?

Reading · one page

Epictetus, Enchiridion 1. Some things are up to us: our judgments, intentions, and actions. Others aren’t: our bodies, reputations, and other people.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack a week ahead with the question above and a link to the reading.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Try it on a real worry
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. List what worried you this week. Which items were fully up to you?
  2. Is your health in your control? Your job? Where exactly is the line?
  3. If outcomes aren’t up to us, why try at all?
  4. What changes when you aim at your effort rather than the result?
  5. Where could this idea go wrong if it’s taken too far?

Practice this month

Mine / not mine. Once a day, split a worry into the part that’s yours and the part that isn’t. Act only on yours.

Facilitator’s move

“How would you know?” When someone says something is out of their control, ask how they’d tell.

Month 3 of 12 About 90 minutes

Anger

Is it the thing that happened, or what I tell myself about it?

Reading · one paragraph

Epictetus, Enchiridion 5. We aren’t disturbed by things themselves, but by our judgments about them. Optional: a page from Seneca’s On Anger.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Ask people to notice one moment of irritation during the week.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Aloud, together
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Rewrite one judgment
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Recall a recent moment of anger. What happened, and what did you tell yourself about it?
  2. Seneca thought anger is never useful. Do you agree?
  3. What’s the difference between anger and a firm, fair response?
  4. What would a pause change: counting, walking away, sleeping on it?
  5. When you’re angry, who is harmed most?

Practice this month

Name the judgment. When you’re annoyed, finish the sentence “I’m telling myself that…” Then ask whether it’s true.

Facilitator’s move

“Can you give an example?” Talk about anger stays vague until someone describes a real moment.

Month 4 of 12 About 90 minutes

Worry About the Future

Do we suffer more from what happens, or from what we imagine?

Reading · two pages

Seneca, Letter 13 (“On Groundless Fears”). Many fears never arrive; Seneca asks whether a feared evil is certain before we suffer it.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the question above. Link the letter; reading it ahead is optional.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Key passages aloud
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Five quiet minutes
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. What future event takes up the most room in your head right now?
  2. Think of past worries. How many happened the way you feared?
  3. Seneca asks whether the evil is certain. Is that comforting, or dismissive?
  4. What’s the difference between planning and worrying?
  5. How could calmly imagining the worst help rather than hurt?

Practice this month

Rehearse a setback. For five minutes, calmly picture something going wrong, and plan how you’d respond well.

Facilitator’s move

“If that’s true, what follows?” Walk a worry forward, step by step, to see where it really leads.

Month 5 of 12 About 90 minutes

Difficult People

How do I stay decent toward people who make it hard?

Reading · one paragraph

Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1. Each morning he expects to meet rude, ungrateful people, reminds himself they act from ignorance, and that we’re made to work together.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack. Remind people to keep examples anonymous: no names, no workplaces.

Open · 10Ground rules; keep it anonymous
Read · 10Aloud, twice
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Write your own 2.1
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Marcus expects difficult people every morning. Is that pessimistic or practical?
  2. Think of someone who frustrates you. What might they believe that makes their behavior make sense to them?
  3. Does understanding someone mean excusing them?
  4. In that relationship, what is actually in your control?
  5. What does justice ask of you toward people you don’t like?

Practice this month

Morning preparation. Before work, say your own version of 2.1: who you might meet today, and how you want to treat them.

Facilitator’s move

“Is that always true?” When someone says “people like that never change,” invite a counterexample.

Month 6 of 12 About 90 minutes

What’s Actually Good?

If money, health, and reputation aren’t the goal, what is?

Reading · three pages

Plato, Euthydemus 278e–282e. Socrates argues that wealth and health only benefit us when they’re used wisely, so wisdom is what makes anything good.

Before the meeting

Announce on your Substack with the question above and a link to the reading.

Open · 10Ground rules; welcome newcomers
Read · 10Key passages aloud
Discuss · 45The questions below
Practice · 15Try it on a real choice
Close · 10One takeaway each

Discussion questions

  1. Name three things you want most. Why do you want them?
  2. Can wealth or health ever harm someone? How?
  3. Socrates says wisdom is what makes other things useful. Do you agree?
  4. The Stoics called money and health “preferred indifferents.” What’s gained and lost in that view?
  5. Looking back on six meetings, what’s one idea you’ve actually used?

Practice this month

The wise-person test. Before a decision, ask: what would a wise person do here, and why?

Facilitator’s move

“Is that in your control?” Bring back month two: are the things you want most up to you?

Milestones

After month three: three meetings held. If you also have a co-facilitator, you're eligible to become a member stoa.

By month six: a co-facilitator to share hosting and prep, and to lead when you can't. Ask a regular. Most people say yes when asked directly.

Months Seven to Twelve

The second half of the year goes deeper into the readings and into daily practice. Each meeting follows the same format as the cards above.

Month Seven A Deeper Introduction to Stoicism

What did the Stoics actually believe, and why?

  • Read: the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on Stoicism. It's a little dense, but rich with information and quite readable. Too long for one meeting? Pick one or two sections to focus on.
  • Announce next month that the group will read How to Be a Stoic in months nine and ten, so members have time to get a copy.
  1. Which idea in the article surprised you most?
  2. The Stoics tied ethics to their physics and logic. Does how you see the world change how you should live?
  3. Which idea from our first six months does the article help explain?
  4. What would you still push back on?
  • Practice: pick one sentence from the article that matters to you, and reread it each morning.
  • Facilitator's move: “What do you mean by…?” Words like nature and reason meant something specific to the Stoics.

Month Eight The Three Disciplines

How do you practice Stoicism, not just think about it?

  • Read: so far you've approached Stoicism through the four cardinal virtues. Epictetus taught through a different lens: the three disciplines. Read Donald Robertson's Introduction to Stoicism: The Three Disciplines. For specific daily practices, add his A Simplified Modern Approach to Stoicism.
  1. Desire, action, and judgment: which discipline do you most need right now?
  2. Which practices from earlier months fit under each discipline?
  3. What gets in the way of daily practice for you?
  4. How could this group help you keep at it?
  • Practice: choose one daily practice from Robertson's simplified approach and keep it all month.
  • Facilitator's move: “Can you give an example?” Ask for a moment this week when one of the disciplines would have helped.

Month Nine Read a Book Together: How to Be a Stoic, Part One

What does a modern Stoic life look like?

  • Read: the first half of Massimo Pigliucci's How to Be a Stoic, which introduces modern Stoic theory and practice through Epictetus' three disciplines.
  1. Which of Pigliucci's examples comes closest to your own life?
  2. Where does a modern reading change what Epictetus meant?
  3. Where do you disagree with the author?
  4. What would you ask him if he were here?
  • Practice: keep a one-line Stoic journal each evening.
  • Facilitator's move: “How would you know?” Test the book's claims against members' experience.

Month Ten Read a Book Together: How to Be a Stoic, Part Two

Which Stoic exercise is worth keeping?

  • Read: the second half of the book. The last chapter is a guide to basic Stoic exercises, a natural bridge into practice-focused meetings.
  1. Which exercise in the final chapter appeals to you most? Which least?
  2. Has your view of Stoicism changed since month one? How?
  3. What does the book get right about living well that we haven't discussed?
  4. Which exercise should we try together next month?
  • Practice: choose one exercise from the final chapter and try it daily.
  • Facilitator's move: “Is that always true?”

Month Eleven A Practice Session

What happens when we practice together, not just talk?

  • Instead of a reading: lead the exercise the group chose last month, such as the evening review, rehearsing a setback, or the view from above. Spend 10–15 minutes doing it together. Find more ideas in our practice resources.
  1. What did you notice during the exercise?
  2. Was it harder or easier than you expected? Why?
  3. Which practices have you kept up this year, and which did you drop?
  4. How could the group support each other's practice between meetings?
  • Practice: keep the exercise going all month, and note one thing it changed.
  • Facilitator's move: “If that's true, what follows?”

Month Twelve Looking Back, Looking Ahead

What has a year of Stoicism changed?

  • Instead of a reading: ask each member to bring one passage from the year that stayed with them, and read them aloud.
  1. Which idea have you used most this year?
  2. What still feels hard to practice?
  3. What kept you coming back?
  4. What should our second year look like?
  • Practice: write a short letter to yourself, as Seneca wrote to Lucilius, to reread a year from now.
  • Facilitator's move: “Is that in your control?”
  • Close: choose a format for year two. Topics and Formats has options.

A stoa still meeting after a year is a success.
