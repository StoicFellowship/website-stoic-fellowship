Facilitator's Guide to Running a Stoa

Practical suggestions for running meetings and keeping your group going. These are suggestions, not requirements. Member stoas agree only to our Terms and Conditions; beyond that, build the group that best serves your community.

Section One Ground Rules

Many facilitators find that laying out a few ground rules at the start of each meeting, and enforcing them gently but firmly, helps the group run smoothly for everyone. For example:

  • Stoicism is a cosmopolitan philosophy, so the group is open to anyone.
  • We're here to explore Stoicism. Stay focused, and avoid drifting into general philosophical or political debates.
  • Use the meeting to practice the four cardinal virtues: wisdom (navigate a difficult discussion wisely), courage (speak up and contribute), justice (treat others fairly and with respect), and temperance (don't speak too little or too much).
  • If you've spoken a lot, pause before chipping in again so others have a chance.
  • The only things not tolerated are intolerance and rudeness, which may lead to temporary or permanent removal from the group.

Section Two Running a Meeting

  • Be the group's facilitator, not its lecturer. Your job is to draw others into the conversation.
  • Let participants briefly introduce themselves. It helps everyone get to know each other.
  • Don't let any one person dominate the discussion. Be polite but firm.
  • If the conversation wanders too far from the topic, gently nudge it back.
  • If someone asks a question you can't answer, say so, as Socrates would. Ask whether anyone else knows, or note it to research and revisit next time.
  • For a ready-made structure (opening, theme, discussion, reflection, and closing), see the sample Stoa Meeting Guide.

Section Three The Socratic Method

The Socratic method, or elenchus (cross-examination), is one of the best ways to run a fruitful, enjoyable discussion in a small group. Rather than making long statements, the facilitator asks probing questions, perhaps mixed with short explanations. A thesis (for example, from the week's reading) is examined, alternatives are proposed and examined in turn. Often the result is aporia, a state of uncertainty that the ancients considered the beginning of wisdom.

  1. Briefly explain how the method works, so participants know what to expect.
  2. After you ask a question, be comfortable with silence. People need time to think and to work up the courage to speak.
  3. Expect some “productive discomfort”: the method may lead people to question their own beliefs.
  4. Use follow-up questions to explore the topic further.
  5. You're not the sage on the stage. You stand to learn from the discussion like everyone else.
  6. Welcome unusual ideas; discourage ones that aren't serious or that pull the group off topic.
  7. Short clarifications are fine. Lectures are not.

Section Four Keeping Your Stoa Going

  • Share the planning, but lead. Most people want to participate, not organize. Ask members for topic ideas, but use your judgment about what will work.
  • Keep it interesting for you, too. Facilitators burn out less when some topics reflect their own interests.
  • Vary topics and voices. Rotate topics and presenters, and invite an outside speaker from time to time, such as a philosophy or history faculty member from a local college.
  • Announce every meeting. Post each meeting on your event platform with a link to RSVP and to any suggested reading.
  • Make room to socialize. If you can, follow the meeting with an informal social. Connection is a big part of why people come.
  • Don't take attendance personally. Some people become regulars; others show up now and then. That's the nature of the thing.
