Foundations

Three Principles

  • Give every meeting a clear focus. A topic, a reading, or a format you repeat. Open-ended meetings tend to wander.
  • Repeat a format. A familiar structure means less planning for you and less uncertainty for newcomers.
  • Ask your members. What they want to explore keeps them coming back, but use your judgment about what will work.
2

Structure

Choose a Format

Many stoas settle into one or more repeatable formats:

  • Topic of the month. Briefly introduce a common problem, such as anger, anxiety, grief, or friendship, and discuss it from a Stoic perspective. See the topic ideas below.
  • A book over several months. Choose an ancient text or a modern introduction from our study resources and read it together a section at a time. Don't bite off more than your group can chew.
  • Essays. Short essays make easy, focused readings. Try Massimo Pigliucci's Figs in Winter or Donald Robertson's articles.
  • Case studies. Bring a real dilemma (with names removed) or a short scenario. Ask: What's in this person's control? Which virtue does it call for? What would you do?
  • Practice sessions. Try a Stoic exercise together and share how it went. See our practice resources for ideas.

Ideas

Topic Ideas

Each topic pairs an everyday problem with a short classic reading. Free translations of Epictetus, Seneca, and Marcus Aurelius are listed in our study resources.

  • What's in my control? Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 · Meeting guide
  • Anger and judgment: Epictetus, Enchiridion 5; Seneca, On Anger 2.4 (the three movements of anger) · Meeting guide
  • Worry about the future: Seneca, Letter 13 · Meeting guides: Worry About the Future, When a Difficulty Becomes a Disaster (PDF)
  • Difficult people: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 2.1; Epictetus, Enchiridion 43 and 45 · Meeting guides: Difficult People, Before You Judge Someone
  • Getting out of bed (work and duty): Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1 and 5.9 · Meeting guide
  • Knowing what to do: Epictetus, Enchiridion 30; Musonius Rufus, Lecture 5 · Meeting guide
  • Doing your part: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.12 and 10.4 · Meeting guide
  • What's actually good? Plato, Euthydemus 278e–282e · Meeting guide
  • Grief and loss: Epictetus, Enchiridion 3 and 11 · Meeting guide
  • Friendship and other people: Seneca, Letters 9 and 48; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.6 · Meeting guide
  • Time and busyness: Seneca, Letter 1 or On the Shortness of Life · Meeting guide
  • Wanting and needing: Epictetus, Enchiridion 2 and 4 · Meeting guide
  • How much is enough? Epictetus, Enchiridion 15; Seneca, Letter 23 · Meeting guide
  • Pleasure and temptation: Epictetus, Enchiridion 34; Discourses 4.12 · Meeting guide
  • Comfort and discomfort: Epictetus, Enchiridion 2; Seneca, Letter 18 · Meeting guide
  • Reputation and what others think: Epictetus, Enchiridion 13; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 12.4 · Meeting guide
  • Death: Epictetus, Enchiridion 21
  • The four virtues: Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 3.6 · Meeting guide
  • Examining impressions: Epictetus, Enchiridion 1; Discourses 3.12; Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.49 · Meeting guides: Examine Your Impressions, When Your Mind Jumps to Conclusions (PDF)
  • Accepting what happens: Epictetus, Enchiridion 8; Seneca, Letter 107 · Meeting guide
  • Building a practice that lasts: Seneca, Letter 20; Epictetus, Discourses 3.2 · Meeting guide (PDF)

Readings

A Reading-Based Path

Prefer to work through readings in order? This sequence goes from a first taste of Stoicism to its theory and practice:

  1. Plato's Euthydemus (278e–282e): why wisdom is the highest good
  2. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on Stoicism
  3. Donald Robertson on Epictetus' three disciplines
  4. Massimo Pigliucci's How to Be a Stoic, over two meetings

Meeting guides with links and discussion questions for each are in Meeting Guides, under Getting Started and Reading Together.
