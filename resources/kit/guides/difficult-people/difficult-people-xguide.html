Expanded Guide: Difficult People

Print or save as PDF

The Stoic Fellowship Meeting Guide · Everyday Problems

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer introduces the readings and key terms, and the meeting guide gives the sequence and timing. This guide explains what each part of the meeting is for and what to listen for.

What this meeting is for

This session picks up the third topic of What Is Stoicism?: the four virtues, especially justice. It also draws on What’s in My Control? and Anger: other people’s behavior isn’t up to us, but our judgment and conduct are.

A participant should leave able to prepare for a hard encounter: anticipate it, understand the other person’s view, and choose how to act. Two points matter most:

  • Understanding isn’t excusing. Seeing why someone acts as they do is accurate seeing, not approval.
  • Decency isn’t doormat. Fair treatment and clear boundaries can go together.

Leading it Socratically

  • Keep it anonymous. No names, no workplaces. The shared case keeps the focus on the practice.
  • Turn complaints into questions. “What is up to you in that relationship?” moves the room from venting to practice.
  • Invite counterexamples. When someone says “people like that never change,” ask whether it’s always true.
  • Put safety first. If someone describes abuse, don’t ask them to understand the abuser. Protection comes first.

The discussion: five questions

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups, and something to listen for. The meeting guide prints the first follow-up as the probe.

1. Marcus expects difficult people every morning. Is that pessimistic or practical?

Purpose: frame preparation as readiness.

  • How might expecting it change your response?
  • What’s the difference between expecting difficulty and expecting the worst of people?

Listen for the difference between bracing for behavior and condemning the person. Marcus expects bad behavior and still calls these people his kin.

2. Think of that person. What might they believe that makes their behavior make sense?

Purpose: practice seeing from another point of view.

  • Does that belief change how you see them?
  • What might they think they’re protecting?

Listen for explanations that stay general and fair. Avoid inventing motives about real, named people.

3. Does understanding someone mean excusing them?

Purpose: separate understanding from approval.

  • Can you hold someone responsible without hating them?
  • What does hatred add to a fair judgment?

Listen for the Stoic answer: we can judge an action wrong, respond firmly, and still refuse hatred.

4. In a difficult relationship, what is genuinely up to you?

Purpose: bring back What’s in My Control?

  • What would a fair boundary look like?
  • What part of the relationship isn’t yours to fix?

Listen for boundaries framed as one’s own conduct: “I’ll leave the room if the criticism starts,” not “They have to stop.”

5. What does justice ask of you toward people you don’t like?

Purpose: connect the meeting to the virtue of justice.

  • Where does fairness end and putting up with harm begin?
  • Is it fair to give someone a chance to change?

Listen for fairness that includes protecting oneself and others. Justice doesn’t require accepting mistreatment.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: Marcus says no one can harm him, because no one can make him act badly. Do you believe that? Let skeptics speak. The claim is about character, not about physical or financial harm.

The practice: morning preparation

A relative criticizes your choices at every family gathering. Another gathering is this weekend, and you’re already rehearsing comebacks.

Read 2.1 aloud again first. Use the case, or a volunteer’s anonymous example. Reveal one step at a time and take two or three answers at each; anyone may say their own version aloud.

1. Prepare. Who might test me, and how? Name the situation, not the person.

Sample response: “At dinner, my relative will ask why I changed jobs and say it was a mistake.”

2. Understand. What might they believe that explains it? Stay general; don’t invent motives.

Sample response: “Maybe they think security matters most, and they’re worried about me.”

3. Choose. How do I want to treat them? Ask for conduct, not feelings.

Sample response: “I’ll answer once, calmly, and change the subject. If it continues, I’ll step out for a few minutes.”

The reveal (if time allows). You learn the relative has been lonely since a spouse died and feels left out of family decisions. What changes in how you see them? What stays the same in how you’ll act? Understanding may soften the judgment of the person; it needn’t change a fair boundary.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“So I just let people walk over me.” Decency means passivity. “What would a fair boundary look like?”
“Understanding them means they’re right.” Understanding is approval. Marcus still judges conduct; he refuses to add hatred.
“Some people are just bad.” Character is fixed. “Is that always true? Can you think of a counterexample?”
“I should feel fine about it.” The practice requires warm feelings. The practice is about conduct, not forced feelings.

Facilitating the people in the room

Who What it looks like Try
The venter Describes a person at length. “What is up to you in that relationship?”
The name‑dropper Identifies someone specific. “Let’s keep it anonymous: what happened, not who.”
The fixer Advises someone how to handle their relative. “What has helped you with someone similar?”
The quiet one Listening but not speaking. Offer an easy entry: “What might the relative believe?”

When an example is painful. Difficult relationships can involve real harm. If someone describes abuse, don’t ask them to understand the abuser; protection comes first. Thank them, don’t press for details, and move to the shared case. Check in privately afterward.

Questions you may be asked

“Didn’t Marcus have power over these people?” He did, which makes his restraint more notable. The practice works at any level: it’s about our own conduct.

“Is this about forgiveness?” Partly. The Stoics thought wrongdoing comes from mistaken judgments, which makes forgiveness easier. They didn’t think it meant ignoring harm.

“Do I need to read anything?” No. Newcomers need no preparation. The Meeting Primer is optional.

Adapting the meeting

  • Mostly newcomers. Use the shared case, and give questions 2 and 3 extra time.
  • Mostly experienced members. Leave room for the going-deeper question.
  • Online. Remind people about anonymity at the start, and put the three steps in the chat.
  • A large group. Try the practice in pairs, then hear one version from each pair.
  • Short on time. Skip the reveal or shorten question 5.
