The Stoic Fellowship Meeting Guide · Getting Started

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer covers the readings and key terms, and the meeting guide covers the flow and timing. This guide covers the heart of the meeting: the practice and the discussion. It explains what each part is for, what to listen for, and what to do when the conversation goes somewhere unexpected.

What the practice and discussion are for

This is often a group's first meeting, and many people in the room will be new to Stoicism. The meeting has one idea to carry: a good life depends on character, not circumstances. The introduction states it, the discussion tests it, and the evening review gives people one way to start living it.

Keep two points in view throughout.

  • Short introduction, long discussion. Keep the talk or video to ten minutes. People learn more by testing the claim than by hearing it explained.
  • The everyday meaning of "stoic" is not the philosophy. Expect it to come up. It's a useful starting point, not a mistake to correct quickly.

Leading it Socratically

A first meeting sets the tone for every meeting after it. Model the habits you want the group to keep.

  • Ask for definitions. When someone says good, happy, or strong, ask what they mean. That's the facilitator's main move this meeting.
  • Let skeptics speak. The central claim is bold. Testing it is the point, and a skeptic gives the room something to work with.
  • Ask for examples. "What would that look like on an ordinary day?" keeps the talk concrete for newcomers.
  • Invite disagreement. "Who sees it differently?" is more useful than "Does everyone agree?"
  • Let questions stay open. A first meeting doesn't need to settle whether character is the only good.

The practice: a one-minute evening review

Try it together on today so far. Give everyone a quiet minute to look back over the day, then take the three questions aloud, one at a time. Small things count.

Take two or three answers at each step; passing is always welcome. No writing is needed.

1. Credit. What went well? Give the quiet minute first. Small things count: a patient reply, a task finished, a walk taken.

2. Notice. What didn't? Ask people to name it plainly, without judging themselves. The aim is to see clearly, not to feel bad.

3. Adjust. What will I do differently tomorrow? Ask for one small, specific change. Two or three people share one answer each.

Sample response: "What went well: I let a coworker finish before I answered. What didn't: I snapped at my kid this morning when we were running late. Tomorrow: I'll get up ten minutes earlier so the morning isn't a rush."

Seneca describes this practice in On Anger 3.36: each night he reviews his day, names his mistakes plainly, pardons himself, and resolves to do better next time.

The discussion: five questions

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups (the meeting guide prints the first), and something to listen for.

1. Before today, what did you think “stoic” meant? What surprised you? Purpose: start from what people bring, and set the everyday meaning beside the philosophy.

  • Which popular meaning is closest to the philosophy, and which is furthest from it?
  • Where did you first hear the word used?

Listen for "unemotional" and "tough." Collect a few meanings, then ask how they compare with what the introduction said.

2. Stoics say a good life depends on character, not circumstances. Do you believe it? Purpose: test the central claim.

  • What would someone who disagrees point to?
  • Can you think of someone whose circumstances were hard but whose life you'd still call good?

Listen for objections about real suffering. The Stoics preferred health and safety too; the claim is about what makes a life good, not what makes it comfortable.

3. Think of someone you admire for how they handle hardship. What do they actually do? Purpose: make the idea concrete with a real person.

  • What do they do, as opposed to what they feel?
  • Which of the four virtues do you see in it?

Listen for actions rather than feelings. Real people make the idea concrete; ask what the person actually does.

4. Wisdom, courage, justice, temperance: which comes easiest to you? Which is hardest? Purpose: introduce the four virtues through people's own experience.

  • Where did you see one of them in someone else recently?
  • How would you describe temperance to someone who has never heard the word?

Listen for confusion about temperance (self-control, moderation) and justice (fairness, care for others, not only the law).

5. What would make a group like this worth your time each month? Purpose: hear what people want from the group, and plan around it.

  • What would make you bring a friend next time?
  • What would you want to try between meetings?

Listen for practical ideas for the group. Note them for planning; don't solve them in the room.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: the Stoics claimed character is the only true good, not just the most important one. What would follow if that were true? This lets experienced members push on the strongest form of the claim without losing newcomers.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“Stoics don't have feelings.” Stoicism means suppressing emotion. The Stoics examined the judgments behind emotions and valued joy, goodwill, and care.
“So money and health don't matter.” Externals are worthless. They can be worth choosing and using well. They don't decide whether you live well.
“Just accept whatever happens.” Stoicism is passive. The Stoics were active in public life. The question is how to act well, not whether to act.
“It's about being tough.” Endurance is the goal. Courage is one virtue of four, and it works alongside wisdom, justice, and temperance.
“The evening review is about beating yourself up.” The practice is self-criticism. Seneca names his mistakes, pardons himself, and resolves to do better.

Facilitating the people in the room

A first meeting draws a mix of people. Each brings something the meeting needs.

Who What it looks like Try
The expert Has read a lot and starts to lecture. “That's helpful. How would you put it for someone hearing it for the first time?”
The newcomer Unsure of the vocabulary and quiet. “Here's the one idea we're exploring today: character over circumstances.”
The skeptic Thinks the central claim is naive. Welcome it: “What would you point to?” Then ask the room to test it.
The life‑hacker Wants Stoicism as a productivity tool. Ask what good means to them, and whether productivity is part of it.
The talker Fills every silence. “Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet.”
The quiet one Listening closely but not speaking. Offer an easy entry: “Which of the four virtues comes easiest to you?” Passing is always fine.

When an example is painful. Talking about hardship can bring up real loss. If someone shares something painful, thank them, and don't press for details. Offer a pause or move on. The group is for philosophy, not therapy; if someone seems to be struggling, check in with them privately afterward.

Questions you may be asked

“Isn't Stoicism about suppressing emotions?” That's the everyday meaning of "stoic," not the philosophy. The Stoics examined emotions and the judgments behind them, and valued joy, goodwill, and care.

“Is Stoicism a religion?” No. The ancient Stoics had views about God and nature, and members today hold many different beliefs. The group focuses on practice.

“Who were the Stoics?” The school began with Zeno of Citium in Athens around 300 BCE. The best-known later Stoics are Seneca, a Roman statesman; Epictetus, a former slave who became a teacher; and Marcus Aurelius, a Roman emperor.

“Do I need to read anything?” No. Newcomers need no preparation. The Meeting Primer is optional.

“What does a regular meeting look like?” Most meetings follow today's shape: a short reading, a practice, and a discussion around one question.

Adapting the practice and discussion

  • Mostly newcomers. Keep the introduction short, and use questions 1, 2, and 3.
  • Mostly experienced members. Shorten the introduction, and leave room for the going-deeper question.
  • Online. Share the video on screen, call on people by name, and remind them they may pass.
  • A large group. Run the evening review in pairs, then hear one answer from each pair.
  • Short on time. Skip question 4 or 5.
