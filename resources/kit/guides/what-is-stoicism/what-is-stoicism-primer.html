What Is Stoicism?

To most people, "stoic" means keeping a stiff upper lip: enduring hardship without complaint and without showing much feeling. The philosophy is something else.

Stoicism is a school of philosophy founded in Athens around 300 BCE. Its central claim is that a good life depends on character, not circumstances: on wisdom, courage, justice, and temperance, which no one can take from us. Health, money, and reputation can still be worth pursuing, but they don't decide whether we live well. The Stoics didn't ask us to stop feeling. They asked us to examine the judgments behind our feelings, and they valued joy, goodwill, and care for others.

The readings below give you the key ideas in their own words. For ancient texts, use any translation you like.

Optional Reading (about 10 minutes)

  1. Stoicism 101 (5 minutes) The Stoic Fellowship's short introduction. As you read, notice which ideas match what you thought "stoic" meant, and which don't.

  2. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (2 minutes) Epictetus opens his handbook here: some things are up to us, and some are not. Which items on his two lists surprise you?

  3. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (1 minute) People are disturbed not by things but by their judgments about things. How does that change what "being stoic" might mean?

  4. Seneca, On Anger 3.36 (2 minutes) Seneca describes reviewing his day each night. This is where our practice comes from. What does he ask himself, and how does he treat his own mistakes?

Think About

In the meeting, we'll try a one-minute evening review together. Each question below lines up with one of its three steps. Carry them with you; you don't need to write anything down.

  • Credit. What went well today, however small?
  • Notice. What didn't go well? Can you name it without judging yourself?
  • Adjust. If you could change one small thing tomorrow, what would it be?

Key Terms

For reference during the meeting. No need to memorize these.

Stoicism. From the stoa, a painted porch in Athens where Zeno of Citium taught around 300 BCE.

Virtue (aretē). Excellence of character. For the Stoics, the one thing that is truly good.

The four virtues. Wisdom, courage, justice, and temperance (self-control). The Stoics saw them as four sides of one excellent character.

Character, not circumstances. Stoicism's central claim: whether we live well depends on how we judge and act, not on what happens to us.

Up to us (eph' hēmin). What depends on our own judgment: our opinions, desires, and choices (Enchiridion 1). Other people, our bodies, and outcomes are not fully up to us, though they can still deserve care and effort.

Evening review. Looking back over the day to see your own conduct clearly and choose one thing to do better. Seneca describes it in On Anger 3.36.
