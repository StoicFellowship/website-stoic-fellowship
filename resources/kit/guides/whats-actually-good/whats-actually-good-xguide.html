Expanded Guide: What’s Actually Good?

The Stoic Fellowship Meeting Guide · Everyday Problems

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer introduces the readings and key terms, and the meeting guide gives the sequence and timing. This guide explains what each part of the meeting is for and what to listen for.

What this meeting is for

This session picks up the first topic of What Is Stoicism?: a good life, and how much it depends on circumstances. It closes the first half of the Guided Year, so question 5 looks back.

A participant should leave able to explain the difference between what’s good and what’s merely preferred, and to test a real choice against it. Two points matter most:

  • Preferred, not worthless. The Stoics chose health and income when they could. The claim is about what makes a life good, not that comfort doesn’t matter.
  • Use is what counts. Socrates’ argument turns on how things are used. That keeps the discussion practical.

Leading it Socratically

  • Ask “Is that in your control?” Bring back What’s in My Control?: most of the things people want most aren’t fully up to them.
  • Ask for examples. An advantage that did harm makes the argument real.
  • Welcome objections. “This ignores real suffering” is a fair challenge. The Stoics preferred health and security too.
  • Avoid moralizing. Money isn’t bad. The question is what makes the difference.

The discussion: five questions

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups, and something to listen for. The meeting guide prints the first follow-up as the probe.

1. Can wealth or health ever harm someone? How?

Purpose: test Socrates’ premise with real cases.

  • What made the difference: the thing, or how it was used?
  • Is there anything that can’t be misused?

Listen for examples where an advantage did harm. That’s Socrates’ argument in people’s own words.

2. Socrates says wisdom makes other things useful. Do you agree?

Purpose: examine the conclusion.

  • Can you think of an advantage that did harm in unwise hands?
  • Could wisdom itself ever do harm?

Listen for the Stoic answer to the second follow-up: wisdom, rightly understood, can’t be misused, which is why they called it good.

3. Why call money and health “preferred indifferents”?

Purpose: introduce the key terms.

  • What does “preferred” keep that “indifferent” might seem to lose?
  • How would you explain the idea to a friend?

Listen for both words being taken seriously. “Indifferent” doesn’t mean “don’t care”; “preferred” means it’s reasonable to choose them.

4. If wisdom is the real good, what would you do differently?

Purpose: bring the idea into practice.

  • What choice would you make for a different reason?
  • What would you stop chasing?

Listen for small, concrete changes rather than grand renunciations.

5. What’s one idea from our meetings you’ve actually used?

Purpose: consolidate the first half of the year.

  • What happened when you used it?
  • Which idea would you most like to keep practicing?

Listen for the practices: the evening review, mine and not mine, catching the second movement, rehearsing a setback, morning preparation. First-timers can answer with an idea from today.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: Could a wise person be happy while poor or seriously ill? What would the Stoics say, and do you agree? The Stoics said yes, because happiness depends on character. Many people disagree; let both sides speak.

The practice: the wise-person test

You’re offered a job with much better pay. It means longer hours and frequent travel, and you’d see less of the people you care about.

Use the case, or a volunteer’s real choice if one is ready. Reveal one step at a time and take two or three answers at each.

1. Want. What do I want here, and why? Name the wants honestly; there are no wrong answers.

Sample response: “More money and security. Some recognition, too.”

2. Ask. What would a wise person do, and why? Ask people to picture someone they consider wise.

Sample response: “She’d ask what the money is for, and whether the job lets her keep her commitments.”

3. Let go. What would I have to let go of? Either choice gives something up.

Sample response: “If I take it, time at home. If I don’t, some security I’d like to have.”

The reveal (if time allows). The offer also comes with a title you’ve wanted for years. Does the title change what a wise person would do? Why or why not? Reputation is another preferred indifferent; ask whether it changes the reasoning or just the pull.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“So money doesn’t matter.” Indifferent means worthless. It matters as a preferred indifferent: worth choosing, worth using well.
“The Stoics wanted us to be poor.” Stoicism rejects comfort. Seneca was wealthy. The question is what makes a life good.
“This ignores real suffering.” Preferred indifferents are trivial. “The Stoics preferred health and security too.”
“Good means pleasant.” Good is a feeling. For the Stoics, good means what always benefits.

Facilitating the people in the room

Who What it looks like Try
The skeptic Thinks the view is unrealistic. “What would you point to?” Then ask the room to test it.
The moralizer Condemns wanting money. “Money isn’t bad either. What makes the difference?”
The abstract thinker Debates definitions. “Can you give an example from this week?”
The first-timer New today, unsure about question 5. “What idea from today might you use?”

When an example is painful. Talk of money and health can touch real hardship. If someone shares something painful, thank them, don’t press for details, and move on. The group is for philosophy, not therapy.

Questions you may be asked

“Is this Stoic or Platonic?” Both. The argument is Socrates’, in a dialogue by Plato. The Stoics took Socrates as a model and built their view of good and indifferent on arguments like this.

“Why call health ‘indifferent’?” Because it can be used well or badly, so it doesn’t make a life good by itself. It’s still reasonable to prefer it.

“Do I need to read anything?” No. Newcomers need no preparation. The Meeting Primer is optional.

Adapting the meeting

  • Mostly newcomers. Use the shared case, and give questions 1 and 3 extra time.
  • Mostly experienced members. Use a volunteer’s real choice, and leave room for the going-deeper question.
  • Online. Put the three steps in the chat.
  • A large group. Try the practice in pairs, then hear one answer from each pair.
  • Short on time. Skip the reveal or shorten question 4; keep question 5.
