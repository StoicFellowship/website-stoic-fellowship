Guiding Question: Do we suffer more from what happens, or from what we imagine?

At the Meeting

  • Today’s reading: Seneca, Letters 13.4–8. We’ll read key passages aloud together.
  • The practice: Rehearse a Setback, in three steps.
    1. Picture it. Calmly imagine one thing going wrong.
    2. Plan. How would I respond well?
    3. Release. Let it go and return to what is happening now.

Worry About the Future

A presentation is a week away. You picture losing your place, the room going quiet, someone looking at their phone. By the time it arrives, you’ve lived through it a dozen times, and it went badly every time.

Seneca wrote to a worried friend that there are more things likely to frighten us than to crush us, and that we suffer more often in imagination than in reality. He suggests a simple test: is the feared evil certain? Many fears never arrive. Some do, and Seneca doesn’t pretend otherwise. His point is that suffering in advance helps with neither.

The Stoics also had a constructive use for imagining the worst. Premeditatio malorum, the premeditation of adversity, means calmly picturing a setback and planning how you’d respond well. Done briefly and calmly, it turns worry into preparation.

For ancient texts, use any translation you like.

Optional Reading (about 8 minutes)

  1. Seneca, Letters 13.4–8 (4 minutes) This is the passage we’ll read from. How does Seneca examine a forecast rather than automatically accept it?

  2. Seneca, Letters 24.1–2 (2 minutes) A friend fears the outcome of a lawsuit. What surprising advice does Seneca give him?

  3. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (1 minute) We are disturbed by our judgments about things. How does that apply to things that haven’t happened yet?

Think About

We’ll rehearse a shared case aloud. You can use a made-up example.

  • Picture it. What’s one thing that could go wrong this month? Can you picture it calmly?
  • Plan. How would you respond well if it happened?
  • Release. Once you have a plan, what helps you put the worry down?

Key Terms

For reference during the meeting. No need to memorize these.

Premeditation of adversity (premeditatio malorum). Calmly rehearsing possible setbacks so you’re ready to respond well.

Impression (phantasia). How something appears to us, including a picture of the future. We can examine an impression before we accept it.

Assent (synkatathesis). Agreeing that an impression is true. A forecast is an impression; we needn’t assent to it.
