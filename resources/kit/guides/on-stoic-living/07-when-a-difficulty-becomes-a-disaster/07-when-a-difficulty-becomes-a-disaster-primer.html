Guiding Question: What is the difficulty—and what am I predicting it means?

At the Meeting

  • Today’s reading: Seneca, Letters 13.4–8. We’ll bring it into discussion question 2.
  • The shared case: A possible unanswered question becomes a prediction that an entire argument will be dismissed. Everyone can reason from these facts, whether or not they have done the weekly practice.

When a Difficulty Becomes a Disaster

You’re preparing to speak about a proposal affecting your street. A question you cannot answer could come up. Before anyone has asked it, you imagine losing your credibility and start to feel that you must sound certain.

July brings courage to the Discipline of Assent. A difficulty can make a claim feel convincing because it concerns something we care about. We examine the prediction, the importance we give the possible loss, and any poor choice the prediction starts to make acceptable.

A serious risk deserves accurate attention. The examination might confirm it. It might also show that one unanswered question would leave other points well supported. Preparing a judgment helps you recognize the distinction when pressure arrives. The actual question may still reveal something you need to correct.

The readings connect anticipation with careful judgment. For ancient texts, use any translation you like.

Optional Reading (about 8 minutes)

  1. Seneca, Letters 13.4–8 (3 minutes) This is the passage we’ll read together. What does Seneca ask Lucilius to establish before treating an anticipated trouble as something he must bear?

  2. Epictetus, Enchiridion 4 (1 minute) What does Epictetus want to preserve if his outing is disrupted? How could preparation keep a claim from becoming persuasive through surprise alone?

  3. Epictetus, Enchiridion 9 (1 minute) What does Epictetus distinguish about a bodily impediment and the use of choice? What would that clarify without denying a practical limit?

  4. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.21 (3 minutes) What would Marcus need someone to show him before he changed his mind? How can courage include being willing to revise?

Think About

The shared case is enough to participate. We explore reasons and alternatives before using the application steps.

  • What is possible in the case, and what has been treated as certain?
  • What does fear make sounding certain seem to permit?
  • How should new criticism change the judgment you prepared?

Application at the Meeting

Application after discussion: Test What You Fear. Use the steps once to test the understanding developed together.

1. **Separate.** What is happening or possible, and what am I adding?
2. **Test.** What supports the forecast and what it seems to justify?
3. **Prepare.** What judgment can I endorse and check when the difficulty comes?

Compare “I may not know an answer” with “None of my points will count.” Use Separate → Test → Prepare once to state a judgment with reasons. Add the conflicting figure if useful and reconsider how much of the argument it affects.

Success is a better-grounded judgment, including a serious revision or uncertainty. A confident performance, diminished fear, or invented reassurance is not the test.

Key Terms

For reference during the meeting. No need to memorize these.

Impression (phantasia). How a situation appears, including a prediction or interpretation that may seem settled before examination.

Assent (synkatathesis). Accepting a claim as true or warranted. The strength of apprehension cannot establish the claim by itself.

Bracketing. Separating what is observed or reported from what you have added about it.

Prediction. A claim about what may happen. Imagining an event does not supply evidence that it will occur.

Premeditation of Adversity. Preparing for a plausible difficulty and examining the judgment it could make persuasive. This is distinct from deciding that the event is certain.

Faculty of choice (prohairesis). Our capacity to examine impressions and form judgments and intentions. A threatened reputation does not determine how we use it.

Dispreferred indifferents. Unwelcome externals that may deserve avoidance or care without being moral evils. Their practical costs can be real.

Confidence. In this training, confidence rests on understanding what the difficulty can and cannot establish about living well.

The Discipline of Assent. Training the judgments we endorse. July’s question is How can I judge clearly when difficulty makes itself felt?
