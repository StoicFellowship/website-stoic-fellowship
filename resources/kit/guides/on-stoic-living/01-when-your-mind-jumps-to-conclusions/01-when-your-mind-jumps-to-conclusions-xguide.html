Expanded Guide: When Your Mind Jumps to Conclusions

Print or save as PDF

On Stoic Living Discussion Series · Guide 1 of 12 · January

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer and the core reading (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer covers the readings and key terms, and the meeting guide covers the flow and timing. This guide covers the heart of the meeting: the practice and the discussion. It explains what each part is for, what to listen for, and what to do when the conversation goes somewhere unexpected.

What the practice and discussion are for

January trains one skill: noticing a conclusion as a conclusion, and giving it no more confidence than its grounds support. The practice rehearses that skill on a shared case. The discussion then asks why it is hard, what belongs to us, and what testing a judgment accomplishes even when the judgment survives.

Keep two points in view throughout. They come up in almost every meeting on this topic.

  • Accuracy, not comfort. A reassuring explanation (“they were probably busy”) needs evidence just as much as a troubling one. The meeting does not teach positive thinking.
  • Withholding assent is not withholding action. Leaving the colleague's motive unsettled doesn't mean doing nothing. You may not know why they haven't replied, but you know you need an answer by tomorrow. Separate What do I know? from What should I do, given what I know?

Members following On Stoic Living will have spent four weeks on this: the Fundamental Rule (Week 1), the Stoic Pause (Week 2), Bracketing (Week 3), and Examine Your Impressions (Week 4). The email case comes from Week 4, so some may recognize it. That helps: they can say what they noticed during the month while newcomers meet the case fresh.

Leading it Socratically

This topic suits a Socratic meeting because the practice itself is a form of questioning. Your job is to model the move, not to explain it.

  • Ask for reasons, not agreement. When someone offers a conclusion, ask what supports it. Ask the same of a reassuring one. The symmetry is the lesson.
  • Restate before you test. “So you're saying the silence means they've seen it?” lets the speaker correct you and slows the room down.
  • Use the case as common ground. Personal examples are welcome, but the email case lets everyone test a judgment without exposing their own. Return to it whenever the conversation drifts.
  • Invite disagreement. “Who reads this differently?” is more useful than “Does everyone agree?”
  • Let uncertainty stand. If the group ends a question unsure, that is often the right result. “We don't know yet” is a judgment this month teaches, not a failure of the discussion.

The practice: testing one impression

You emailed a colleague this morning. You need an answer by tomorrow. It's late afternoon, and there's no reply. You think, “They're deliberately ignoring me.”

Members will want to jump straight to solving the problem (“just call them”). Note the idea and hold it for step 3. At each step, take two or three answers before moving on.

1. Pause. Ask someone to name the impression as a claim: “I have the impression that they're deliberately ignoring me.” The point is to hear it as something that could be true or false. If the group skips this, ask, “What exactly is the impression telling you?”

2. Separate. Build two short lists aloud.

What we know What we're adding
The email was sent this morning. They've seen it.
There's no reply yet. They chose not to answer.
The answer is needed by tomorrow. It's about me.

If someone adds “they're probably busy” to the left column, ask whether that is known or added. It goes on the right too.

3. Weigh. Ask how much certainty the known column justifies. Most groups land on leaving the motive open. Then ask what is reasonable to do: a short follow-up that asks for the answer without accusing anyone.

Sample response: “I know I sent the email and still need an answer. I haven't heard back. ‘They saw it’ and ‘they meant to ignore me’ are claims I'd have to check. A busy day is possible too. For now, I can say the reply is still outstanding.”

The reveal (if time allows). Add new evidence: the colleague acknowledged the email this morning and promised a reply by noon. It's now late afternoon. Ask what the group can now say with more confidence, and what remains open.

The point is that better evidence strengthens part of the judgment without settling the motive. A careful test may confirm a first judgment, qualify it, or leave it unsettled.

Sample response: “They acknowledged the message and missed the time they named. I can now say they received it and didn't reply by noon. I still don't know why.”

Follow up: what could a short message say without claiming more than you know?

The discussion: five questions

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups (the meeting guide prints the first), and something to listen for.

1. Why can a first impression feel like something you already know? Purpose: name the problem. Impressions arrive looking like facts.

  • Which came first: the observation, the feeling, or the explanation?
  • If it felt just as convincing tomorrow, what would that tell you about its truth?

Listen for the difference between feeling certain and having grounds. If someone says the feeling is the evidence, ask what else could produce the same feeling.

2. What belongs to your judgment here, and what depends on someone else? Purpose: apply the Fundamental Rule to the case.

  • If the deadline matters, what can you responsibly do while the motive is unsettled?
  • Which part of the situation can you influence, and which judgment can you examine yourself?

Listen for “control” used loosely. You can influence whether a colleague replies, but the reply is theirs. The judgment about their silence is yours. Things not up to us can still deserve care and effort.

3. What would make deliberate avoidance more likely than ordinary delay? Purpose: practice asking what evidence would actually decide the question.

  • If a kind explanation came to mind first, what evidence would you ask of it?
  • What would show they saw the message? What more would tell you why they waited?

Listen for a list of real evidence: a read receipt, a pattern of missed replies, other messages answered. The aim is to see how much more it would take to know a motive.

4. How much certainty does the evidence support? What can you leave open? Purpose: the Week 4 test, applied directly.

  • What one piece of new information would change your confidence the most?
  • Which part of the explanation could you accept provisionally, and on what grounds?

Listen for answers that are neither “certain” nor “no idea.” A firm conclusion, a tentative one, and an unsettled one can each be reasonable.

5. If your first judgment still stands, what did testing it accomplish? Purpose: make clear that examination isn't a way to prove ourselves wrong.

  • How would you tell a sound first judgment from a lucky guess?
  • What reason could you give someone who started with a different impression?

Listen for the idea that a tested judgment is held for reasons, which makes it easier to act on and easier to revise if new evidence arrives.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: what could justify a firm judgment about a pattern of missed replies while leaving the motive for any one delay uncertain? This helps experienced members see that examining impressions is not the same as refusing to conclude anything.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“So I should assume the best.” The practice swaps a gloomy story for a cheerful one. “What supports that? Could the facts fit more than one explanation?”
“So I can't do anything until I know why.” Withholding assent means withholding action. “What does the deadline give you reason to do while the motive is open?”
“So I'm not supposed to feel annoyed.” The practice suppresses feelings. Distinguish the first sting, which isn't up to you, from the judgment that follows, which is yours to examine.
“You can never really know anything.” All interpretations are equally good. “Which explanation has better grounds? What would change your mind?”
“But sometimes people really are ignoring you.” Examination assumes the judgment is wrong. Agree. A test can confirm a judgment. The goal is confidence that matches the evidence.
“I only noticed afterward.” The practice failed. It counts. Ask what they knew at the time and what still deserves that confidence.
“This is just positive thinking.” The practice is a mood technique. It aims at accuracy, not comfort. A comforting story needs grounds too.

Facilitating the people in the room

A few familiar types show up on this topic. Each brings something the meeting needs, if you can steer it.

Who What it looks like Try
The mind reader Confident about others' motives: “I know exactly why they did that.” Thank them for a clear example, then bracket it with the group: “What did we see, and what are we adding?”
The optimist Hurries to the kind explanation and treats the question as settled. Ask what you'd ask of a gloomy story: “What supports that?”
The skeptic Concludes that nothing can be known, so nothing can be done. Separate the two questions: what we know, and what we should do given what we know.
The expert Knows the Greek terms and starts to lecture. Welcome it briefly, then widen it: “How would you say that to someone who's new tonight?”
The problem solver Wants to fix the colleague situation. “Good idea. Before we decide what to do, which judgment are we testing?”
The talker Fills every silence. “Let's hear from someone who hasn't spoken yet.” A quick go-around helps.
The quiet one Listening closely but not speaking. Offer a low-stakes entry: “Which of the two lists would you add to?” Passing is always fine.
The newcomer Unsure of the vocabulary. Define impression and assent in a sentence each as they come up. The case needs no background.

When an example is painful. Jumping to conclusions often involves people we care about. If someone shares a real and painful example, thank them, and don't press for details or test their judgment in front of the group. Offer a pause, return to the email case, or move on. The group is for philosophy, not therapy; if someone seems to be struggling, check in with them privately afterward.

Questions you may be asked

“Is the Stoic Pause actually ancient?” The name is modern. The move is Epictetus's: he describes asking an impression to wait while he examines it (Discourses 2.18.24). A slow breath is a modern aid.

“Isn't this just cognitive behavioral therapy?” They're related. Founders of modern cognitive therapy credited Epictetus's point that we're disturbed by our judgments about things. The aims differ: the Stoics weren't treating a disorder but trying to live wisely, and they cared whether a judgment was true, not only whether it was helpful.

“Does Stoicism say emotions are bad?” Not as people usually mean it. The first jolt isn't up to us and isn't a fault. The Stoics criticized emotions built on mistaken judgments, such as anger that follows from “they're ignoring me” when the evidence doesn't support it. This month examines that judgment.

“What if my first impression was right?” Then the test confirms it, and you hold it for good reasons rather than by luck. That's question 5.

“Isn't ‘up to us’ just the dichotomy of control?” Close, but narrower. “Control” suggests anything we can influence. Epictetus's line runs around our own judgments, desires, and intentions.

Adapting the practice and discussion

  • Mostly newcomers. Give the practice more time, and use questions 1, 2, and 4.
  • Mostly experienced members. Shorten the case, ask what people noticed during the month, and leave room for the going-deeper question.
  • Online. Type the two lists where everyone can see them, call on people by name, and remind them they may pass.
  • A large group. Run the three steps in pairs or threes for ten minutes, then hear one answer from each group.
  • Short on time. Skip the reveal or question 3.
