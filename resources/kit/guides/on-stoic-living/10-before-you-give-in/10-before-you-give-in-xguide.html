Expanded Guide: Before You Give In

Print or save as PDF

On Stoic Living Discussion Series · Guide 10 of 12 · October

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer and the core reading (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer introduces the readings and terms. The meeting guide provides timing. This guide helps you examine the reason an attractive moment seems to supply and test it against a judgment made beforehand.

What the practice and discussion are for

October practices temperance through Assent. A pleasure can seem to carry its own permission. The group pauses to hear the claim, compares what the attractive moment leaves out, and asks whether an exception has sound grounds.

The shared case comes from Week 41. Another episode would be enjoyable. The earlier plan also has a purpose: being ready for a commitment. Both need a fair hearing before the attraction is allowed to settle the choice.

Week 40 identifies the claim accompanying an urge. Week 41 strengthens the pause through delay and comparison. Week 42 examines a claim to priority over current attention. Week 43 prepares a rule with its reason and tests exceptions. These remain parts of one examination.

The group can prepare a simple rule aloud for the case, such as stopping after the episode before an early commitment. Ask what makes that rule worth keeping. In the actual exercise, the reason has to remain available when the next episode appears.

Progress concerns the grounds for keeping or revising the judgment. Strong resistance, a fading attraction, or visible refusal alone cannot show that the reason was sound. Pleasure can remain attractive after the person has decided well.

Leading it Socratically

  • Give enjoyment its due. Ask what the next episode offers before considering what else matters.
  • Hear the actual claim. “This would be enjoyable” differs from “I have reason to continue now.” Ask what supplies the additional ground.
  • Compare the whole choice. Include likely aftermath and the value of remaining faithful to a justified responsibility.
  • Ask what attention would displace. A new episode attracts notice; the earlier commitment may be absent from the screen but still matter.
  • Test a proposed exception. Ask what changed in the circumstances and whether it affects the reason.
  • Examine the rule too. A rule that serves vanity or an arbitrary restriction needs reconsideration even if the person keeps it consistently.

Read Enchiridion 34 aloud. The passage gives restraint a strong recommendation. Ask what makes abstaining worth choosing in the example. When applying the comparison, keep the moral value of the choice distinct from how triumphant restraint might feel.

The practice: pausing, comparing, and testing

An episode ends on a cliffhanger. You planned to stop because you have an early commitment tomorrow. The next episode is ready. You think, “One more would be a good way to end the day.”

Take two or three answers at each step. Participants can use the same character throughout. No one needs to disclose a private habit.

1. Pause. What is the attraction asking me to believe? Hear the promise as a claim. Ask what small action could create room before agreeing. Someone could step away from the screen briefly. In the meeting, discuss that pause and use a few quiet seconds.

Sample response: “It would be enjoyable, and I have added that enjoyment makes continuing a good choice now. I can let the next episode wait while I examine that.”

Delay has a purpose. Replaying the cliffhanger for several minutes may keep the same claim alive without examining it. Ask what the interval would make possible.

2. Compare. What would I enjoy, regret, or value in declining? Ask for the enjoyment, its foreseeable aftermath, and the satisfaction of keeping a sound judgment. Keep the consequences proportionate to the facts.

Sample response: “I'd enjoy the next episode. I'd go to bed later and could be less rested. The reason to stop concerns being ready for people counting on me. Keeping that choice is worth valuing.”

A tired morning alone does not establish moral fault. Ask whether the person had accepted enjoyment as a sufficient reason to disregard a responsibility. Likewise, feeling proud of refusing does not establish that the refusal was justified.

3. Test. Does my reason still hold, or have relevant circumstances changed? Let the group say the prepared reason in one sentence. Then compare it with the exception now being proposed.

Sample response: “The early commitment still stands. The cliffhanger makes watching more attractive, but it hasn't changed the reason to be rested. I have reason to keep my plan.”

Someone might propose a different rule. Ask what it would serve and whether the current occasion gives reason to apply it. A weak rule should be reconsidered thoughtfully, beyond the pressure of the tempting moment.

The reveal (if time allows). You learn that tomorrow’s early commitment has been moved to the afternoon. The original reason now has a changed fact to consider.

Sample response: “The commitment no longer requires the same early start. I need to check whether another reason still supports stopping. A relevant change may justify an exception.”

Have one participant defend keeping the plan and another defend an exception. Both should give a reason. If the broader rule itself needs revision, consider that separately from this particular change in conditions. The earlier decision supplies guidance, and its purpose remains answerable to sound judgment.

The discussion: five questions

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups, and something to listen for. The first follow-up is the booklet's probe.

1. What claim arrives with the wish to keep watching?

Purpose: distinguish an attraction from the judgment that it warrants the choice.

  • Which part of the claim is a reason, and which part describes the attraction?
  • What does the phrase “a good way to end the day” assume about what makes the choice good?

Listen for the leap from expected enjoyment to justified continuation. The claim can be examined without denying that watching is pleasant.

2. What does comparing the enjoyment and aftermath bring into view?

Purpose: make the full choice available while pleasure is vivid.

  • What would make regret concern a poor choice rather than tiredness alone?
  • What satisfaction would belong to keeping a judgment you had good reason to make?

Listen for the responsibility and reason behind the decision. Feelings can support the rehearsal without determining moral worth.

3. What gives this pleasure a claim on your attention now?

Purpose: test apparent priority in relation to what the current choice serves.

  • What responsibility would the next episode displace?
  • When could an enjoyable activity deserve the attention you are giving it?

Listen for reasons for leisure alongside reasons for rest or another commitment. The attraction's arrival alone cannot settle their order.

4. What would justify an exception to your earlier plan?

Purpose: distinguish a relevant changed fact from a stronger pull.

  • What changed besides the strength of the pull?
  • Which part of the rule's purpose would that change actually affect?

Listen for a reason that could be explained outside the tempting moment. The moved commitment changes the case in a way the cliffhanger alone does not.

5. How would you tell sound restraint from keeping a rule for its own sake?

Purpose: keep the prepared standard accountable to its ethical aim.

  • Could a well-grounded revision show better judgment than rigid compliance?
  • What could a perfect record of refusal leave unexamined about the person's purpose?

Listen for the good the rule serves and the reasons supporting its application. A streak is information about repeated behavior, not a complete account of judgment.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: How could the same outward restraint express either good judgment or a wish to look disciplined? Ask what the person would value if no one else knew.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“Pleasure itself is the problem.” Every enjoyable choice is suspect. Ask when the same episode could be a fitting enjoyment.
“The urge proves I need it.” Intensity supplies the missing reason. Ask what the urge claims and what supports it.
“I waited, so I examined it.” Delay completes the work by itself. Ask which claim the interval helped test.
“Regret proves the choice was wrong.” A later feeling settles moral judgment. Ask what responsibility or sound reason was disregarded.
“I must keep any rule I chose.” An earlier decision is beyond revision. Ask what purpose it serves and which facts matter.
“Any new desire justifies an exception.” Increased attraction is a changed justification. Ask what happened to the earlier reason.
“I noticed only afterward.” Hindsight cannot help. Rehearse the claim and comparison using what was known then.

Facilitating the people in the room

Who What it looks like Try
The strict abstainer Equates every refusal with progress. Ask what purpose the restraint serves.
The pleasure defender Hears the exercise as a ban on enjoyment. Give a fitting occasion for the same pleasure and compare its circumstances.
The exception finder Quickly supplies reasons to continue. Ask which new fact changes the prepared reason.
The rule keeper Treats every revision as weakness. Use the reveal to test the scope of the rule.
The self-critic Condemns the whole person after one lapse. Return to the claim accepted and the next occasion to examine it.
The expert Moves into a lecture on passions. Ask how that distinction would affect this choice.
The talker Answers each question before others enter. Invite another willing voice and let the pause stand.
The newcomer Is unsure what assent means. Define it through accepting the next episode as justified now.

When a habit is private. Keep the episode case available. Members can discuss the judgment without naming a personal pattern. Thank anyone who shares and allow passing or a change of example.

Questions you may be asked

“Can the examination support enjoyment?” Yes. A pleasure can fit the circumstances. Its attraction supplies something to consider; the choice needs reasons that account for responsibilities and purpose.

“Are rules Stoic?” The curriculum uses particular rules as guidance grounded in a reason. Seneca connects such guidance with broader ethical principles. The rule keeps the reason available when attraction makes it easy to overlook.

“What if the earlier rule was mistaken?” Reconsider it when you can examine its purpose carefully. Relevant changed conditions can require a different response now. A stronger craving by itself does not establish that the rule's reason was wrong.

“Is noticing an urge already a failure?” No. A felt attraction may be noticed before or after assent. Examine what you have accepted, including an earlier agreement you now need to reconsider.

“Does a distraction always deserve refusal?” The lesson on attention includes genuine interruptions and fitting play. Ask what gives this claim priority in these circumstances.

Adapting the practice and discussion

  • Mostly newcomers. Use the next episode, the earlier plan, and one reason for it. Take the steps slowly.
  • Mostly experienced members. Explore a proposed exception and distinguish changed conditions from revision of the underlying rule.
  • Online. Allow a brief pause, then hear the claim in a sentence before inviting comparison.
  • A large group. Let pairs defend different answers to the reveal, each with a reason.
  • Short on time. Skip the reveal or shorten question 3. Preserve the prepared reason and the test of its exception.

After the meeting, send a short recap through your group's usual channel with one or two observations and the three steps. At the next meeting, invite an example of a claim tested while an attraction remained strong.
