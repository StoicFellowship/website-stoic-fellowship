Guiding Question: What am I treating as something that must never happen?

At the Meeting

  • Today's reading: Epictetus, Enchiridion 2. We'll read it aloud together.
  • The practice: Re-Aim Aversion in three steps.
    1. Name. What external cost do I want to avoid, and why?
    2. Compare. What poor judgment or choice might I accept to escape it?
    3. Re-aim. I would prefer to avoid this cost, but it would be worse to choose this wrong.

What Am I Trying Never to Face?

You borrowed a friend's drill and broke it. You expect an awkward exchange and perhaps a repair bill. Letting your friend think the drill was already faulty begins to seem like an easy way out.

August asks what deserves our aversion. An unwelcome cost can deserve practical attention. The question is how much authority avoiding it has acquired over what we are willing to choose. If avoiding a friend's annoyance makes misleading them seem acceptable, the priority itself needs training.

Re-aiming means becoming more unwilling to choose dishonesty or injustice than to face the cost we hope to escape. We can still seek a fair repair arrangement and prefer a calm conversation. We are practicing a priority that can remain even if the conversation is uncomfortable.

The readings examine aversion, the value of virtue, and the time we have to use well. For ancient texts, use any translation you like.

Optional Reading (about 8 minutes)

  1. Epictetus, Enchiridion 2 (2 minutes) This is the passage we'll read together. Why does Epictetus direct aversion toward what is contrary to nature within our own choice?

  2. Epictetus, Enchiridion 48 (2 minutes) What changes in the progressor's attitude toward praise, criticism, and obstruction as aversion is redirected?

  3. Seneca, On the Happy Life 16 (3 minutes) What gives happiness its basis? How does Seneca distinguish the completely virtuous person from someone beginning to loosen dependence on fortune?

  4. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.17 (1 minute) How does limited time affect the importance of living well now? What condition might keep a worthwhile priority on hold?

Think About

These questions follow the steps. Use the borrowed drill or a made-up example. Nothing needs to be written down.

  • Name. What outcome do you want to avoid? What practical concern gives it weight?
  • Compare. What might you be willing to say, conceal, or neglect to prevent that outcome? If no conflict appears, let that stand.
  • Re-aim. Why would that misuse of choice be worse than the external cost? What would help you mean the priority when the pull toward escape returns?

Key Terms

For reference during the meeting. No need to memorize these.

Aversion (ekklisis). Movement away from what appears bad. Notice both its object and the importance you give preventing it.

External cost. An unwelcome result, such as expense, criticism, or changed plans, that also depends on circumstances or others.

Dispreferred indifferents. Conditions reasonably avoided when appropriate that do not themselves constitute vice. Their practical importance remains.

Virtue and vice. Virtue accords with sound reason and our social nature. Vice is a poor use of judgment and choice, such as deception or injustice.

The Fundamental Rule. Recognizing our own use of choice as distinct from the results and responses we cannot determine.

Two Missions. The modern teaching exercise that gives character priority over an external undertaking. Re-Aim Aversion applies that priority to what we most want to avoid.

The Finite Horizon. A modern reflection on limited time that helps reconsider what deserves priority. It does not predict how long anyone will live.

Voluntary Discomfort. Chosen training in willingness to be without a familiar comfort. August's weekly course uses it as preparation for re-aiming aversion; this meeting requires no discomfort task.

The Discipline of Desire. Training what we seek and avoid. August's question is What deserves my aversion, and what can I learn to face?
