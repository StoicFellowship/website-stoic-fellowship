Guiding Question: What would satisfy the need—and why do I still want more?

At the Meeting

  • Today's reading: Epictetus, Enchiridion 39. We'll read it aloud together.
  • The practice: The Measure of Desire in three steps.
    1. Notice. What does having more seem to promise me?
    2. Measure. Enough for what purpose, and on what terms?
    3. Re-aim. What virtue do I want more than the next addition?

How Much Is Enough?

Your phone served your needs yesterday. Then a friend shows you a newer model, and an upgrade offer makes the familiar one feel inadequate. Has its usefulness changed, or has comparison moved the measure?

November asks how we can want enough without continually needing more. A useful measure starts with what something serves: reliable communication, suitable clothing, rest, or another reasonable purpose. Include real needs and the people affected by the terms of a choice.

The measure helps loosen a demand, but desire needs a direction too. We can come to want wiser and fairer use more than novelty. We can also notice a benefit already present and appreciate it without claiming that it must continue. Keeping an old phone and replacing it can each express sound priorities in different circumstances.

The readings examine a stopping point for wants, receiving what comes, and appreciating what is available. For ancient texts, use any translation you like.

Optional Reading (about 8 minutes)

  1. Epictetus, Enchiridion 39 (2 minutes) This is the passage we'll read together. What supplies the measure in the shoe comparison, and what happens when that standard is replaced by embellishment?

  2. Seneca, Letters 16.7–9 (3 minutes) How does Seneca distinguish limited wants from those that continue indefinitely? What would count as reaching a destination?

  3. Epictetus, Enchiridion 15 (1 minute) What attitude does the banquet image ask for when something is offered, delayed, or passed to someone else?

  4. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 7.27 (2 minutes) Why does Marcus join appreciation of present advantages with a warning against overvaluing them?

Think About

These questions follow the steps. Use the phone case or a made-up example. Nothing needs to be written down.

  • Notice. What do you expect another addition to give you? Which part is a real use, and which part concerns how having it would make you feel?
  • Measure. What would be enough for the purpose, including cost, terms, and responsibilities? What is already serving it?
  • Re-aim. What good use of choice deserves greater desire? How could appreciation remain if the offer passed or the current benefit ended?

Key Terms

For reference during the meeting. No need to memorize these.

The Measure of Desire. A teaching exercise using sufficiency to loosen the demand for more and re-aim desire toward virtue.

Sufficiency. Enough for a reasonable purpose, with regard for needs, relationships, and circumstances.

Desire (orexis). Movement toward what appears good. The exercise asks what we most want from the choice.

Virtue (aretē). Excellence in reason and choice. Possessing a better object cannot supply the wisdom or fairness of using it well.

Indifferents (adiaphora). Externals are neither virtue nor vice in themselves. Their practical value still deserves care.

The Banquet. Epictetus's image for receiving, waiting, and letting an advantage pass without grasping.

Voluntary Simplicity. Testing a perceived need through a simpler arrangement while preserving actual requirements. The meeting uses discussion of that test, not a required trial.

Gratitude. Recognizing a benefit and responding fittingly. A grateful intention can be worthwhile without securing another benefit.

Stoic joy (chara). A rational good feeling directed toward present moral good. We practice appreciating good choice without demanding a cheerful mood.

The Discipline of Desire. Training what we seek and treat as necessary. November's question is How can I want enough without continually needing more?
