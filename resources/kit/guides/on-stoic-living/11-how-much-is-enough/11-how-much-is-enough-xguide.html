Expanded Guide: How Much Is Enough?

Print or save as PDF

On Stoic Living Discussion Series · Guide 11 of 12 · November

For facilitators. Read before the meeting, after the Meeting Primer and the core reading (about 15 minutes).

The Meeting Primer introduces the terms and readings. The meeting guide gives the timing. This guide helps you move from the promise of more to a purpose-based measure and a better direction for desire.

What the discussion and application are for

Hear the promise of novelty and the practical purposes of the phone. Compare measures answerable to needs and terms, then examine how a missed offer, simpler use, and gratitude affect the governing desire. The inquiry concerns sufficiency and desire for wise use, not product selection. Keeping, repairing, or replacing can each be defensible; possession counts and spending totals do not settle the valuation.

November develops temperance through Desire. We ask what an addition promises, set a measure of enough, and rehearse wanting wise and fair use more than the addition itself.

The phone case comes from Week 44. It gives the group an object that serves practical purposes and an offer with terms worth examining. The promise of “free” may obscure a commitment that changes what the household spends. Comparison may also make a suitable phone feel inadequate before any need has changed.

Week 44 sets the measure. Week 45 holds it when an external comes or passes elsewhere. Week 46 tests it in a simpler arrangement. Week 47 notices a benefit already present and practices gratitude without demanding its continuation. The meeting carries those connections through one exercise.

The measure should remain open to evidence of a real need. A person may need a reliable battery or a capability their current device lacks. Recognizing that shortfall can be part of choosing well. The revelation should change the measure where it actually matters.

Progress concerns what the person values. Owning less, saving money, declining an offer, or feeling grateful can be relevant experiences without establishing that desire has shifted toward virtue. Ask what wisdom or justice would mean in how the resource is chosen and used.

Leading it Socratically

  • Hear the promise before setting the limit. Ask what the newer phone seems to settle beyond its practical uses.
  • Start from a purpose. “Enough for what?” gives a measure something definite to answer to.
  • Include the terms. A no-charge device can come with a costly or restrictive agreement. The choice concerns the whole offer.
  • Let real needs correct the measure. Ask what evidence makes an apparent shortfall genuine.
  • Bring the present benefit into view. The current phone’s usefulness can be recognized without claiming it must last forever.
  • Ask what virtue deserves desire. A useful measure supports the ethical aim. The group should still identify what good use they most want.

0–10 minutes — Open and encounter the case

Welcome everyone, explain the discussion agreements, and read the case aloud:

Your phone handles your calls, messages, and ordinary tasks. A friend shows you a newer model. Your carrier offers a “free” upgrade with an unlimited data plan for three years. Your phone now feels inadequate.

Ask the opening question printed in the meeting guide. Hear two or three interpretations without settling them. Carry the differences straight into question 1. Prior weekly experience is welcome, but everyone has the facts needed here; no personal disclosure or practice report is required.

10–60 minutes — Discussion: five questions

Hear the promise of novelty and the practical purposes of the phone. Compare measures answerable to needs and terms, then examine how a missed offer, simpler use, and gratitude affect the governing desire. Hear competing reasons before introducing the full application method. The follow-ups are resources for inquiry, not a script that must all be completed.

Each question has a purpose, two follow-ups, and something to listen for. The first follow-up is the booklet’s probe.

1. What has changed about the phone, and what has comparison added?

Purpose: identify the promise that turns adequacy into felt lack.

  • What do you expect the newer model to provide beyond its practical uses?
  • What would make a better feature relevant to this owner’s circumstances?

Listen for a distinction between a useful improvement and a moving standard of feeling current. Comparison can supply information while still being given more authority than it warrants.

2. What would be enough for the purpose this phone serves?

Read Enchiridion 39 here, when the group seeks a measure of enough. Ask how the shoe’s purpose supplies a limit. Examine how the analogy applies to contemporary needs without assuming every extra capability is excess or the lowest possible provision is sufficient.

Purpose: set a measure answerable to actual needs and responsibilities.

  • Which needs and terms should belong in the measure?
  • What evidence would make the current phone insufficient?

Listen for a concrete purpose and the full commitment of the offer. Different people can need different measures without either being less temperate.

3. What happens to the measure if the offer passes to someone else?

Purpose: hold the measure through receiving, waiting, or missing an advantage.

  • What might you start treating as necessary for a good day?
  • What would remain worth wanting if the offer were no longer available?

Listen for a preference becoming a demand. The opportunity’s disappearance changes what can be acquired. It need not change the purpose or the value of choosing well.

4. What could simpler use and gratitude reveal about what you want?

Purpose: use the month’s testing and appreciation to clarify the same measure.

  • What benefit is already present, and what would a genuine shortfall show?
  • If the current arrangement served the day well, what might still feel missing?

Listen for the contrast between a practical limitation and the desire for novelty or reassurance. Appreciation can bring an overlooked benefit into view while leaving its continuation uncertain.

5. What would show desire shifting toward choosing and using well?

Purpose: locate progress in the governing aim.

  • Could you choose an upgrade while wanting wisdom more than novelty?
  • What would make keeping the old phone equally open to examination?

Listen for what the choice expresses about priorities. A costly purchase can serve a real need. A refusal can be driven by vanity. Ask what reason and desire govern either choice.

Going deeper, for a room that is ready: When could calling something “enough” become a way to dismiss a need that deserves care? Consider whose needs the proposed limit counts.

60–80 minutes — Application: test a measure of enough

Compare a measure based on being up to date with one based on actual uses and shared commitments. Use Notice → Measure → Re-aim once. Add the failing battery to test whether the measure can admit a real need without making novelty the governing aim.

The inquiry concerns sufficiency and desire for wise use, not product selection. Keeping, repairing, or replacing can each be defensible; possession counts and spending totals do not settle the valuation.

Keep sample responses in reserve until participants have offered and questioned their own accounts. Use the method once to test the discussion’s conclusions.

Hear two or three short answers at each step. Everyone can use this invented owner; no participant needs to reveal what they own or spend.

1. Notice. What does having more seem to promise me? Ask the group to distinguish a functional improvement from the feeling of being up to date. Both can be part of the attraction, but their importance needs examination.

Sample response: “I want the newer model to feel caught up. The word ‘free’ also makes it seem that there is little to consider.”

The newer device may have better features. Ask which of them serves a purpose this owner actually has. Do not let the availability of a feature establish its necessity.

2. Measure. Enough for what purpose, and on what terms? Name the uses the phone needs to serve and the costs or commitments that belong in the account.

Sample response: “Enough means reliable calls, messages, and necessary tasks, on terms that suit the household. The current phone appears to meet those needs. I need to consider what the new plan would require.”

Ask what could reveal an unmet need. The owner may need more information before setting a firm measure. A useful answer can be provisional without dissolving into the assumption that any improvement is needed.

3. Re-aim. What virtue do I want more than the next addition? Have someone make wise and fair use concrete. Include appreciation for a present benefit without making that benefit indispensable.

Sample response: “I want to choose wisely and give shared commitments their due. I appreciate that this phone keeps me in touch. I can value using it well without needing the newest model.”

Now imagine that the upgrade offer ends or goes to someone else. What would the owner seem to lose? Return to the same measure. A lost opportunity can disappoint while wise choice remains worth wanting.

The reveal (if time allows). The current phone’s battery has begun failing during trips you need to make alone. A practical limitation now belongs in the account.

Sample response: “Reliability during those trips is part of the purpose. I can investigate repair or replacement. I want the option that serves that need on reasonable terms, rather than assuming novelty is the answer.”

Ask what use of the current phone could test the limitation and what information would distinguish options. Keep this as discussion of the evidence the owner would need. The exercise does not require the group to choose a product.

80–90 minutes — Close

Ask what became clearer or what question remains unsettled. Invite the reason for a changed view, and let a well-supported disagreement remain. End with the single take-home invitation and a brief transition to the next theme. The full method has already appeared during application; do not repeat its steps as an assignment.

Misunderstandings to watch for

If you hear… The misunderstanding Try
“The least possible is always enough.” Sufficiency is measured by deprivation. Ask which actual purpose and need the resource must serve.
“Free means there’s no cost to consider.” One term represents the whole undertaking. Ask what the contract would require and displace.
“Gratitude means I should keep it forever.” Appreciation requires attachment. Ask what can be valued without claiming its continuation.
“The offer passed, so I lost my chance to be content.” An external becomes a condition of living well. Return to what the purpose and a good use still allow.
“More resources show a lack of temperance.” Quantity settles the moral judgment. Ask who needs the resource and how it will be used.
“I thanked someone, so my desire is well directed.” A visible act establishes its underlying aim. Ask what the person wanted from the response.
“I still want it, so nothing changed.” Remaining attraction rules out progress. Ask whether wise use has gained greater importance.

Facilitating the people in the room

Who What it looks like Try
The minimalist Compares how little people need. Ask which purpose gives the limit a reason.
The feature enthusiast Treats every improvement as necessary. Ask what actual use this owner would make of it.
The practical provider Points to a genuine need. Include that evidence and ask how it changes the measure.
The disappointed buyer Treats a missed offer as a ruined occasion. Ask what was lost and what good use remains possible.
The grateful keeper Thinks appreciating something forbids replacement. Distinguish the benefit recognized from a claim to keep it unchanged.
The expert Defines indifferents without considering their use. Ask what the owner’s choice should serve.
The talker Supplies personal shopping stories at length. Return to the shared purpose and invite another willing voice.
The newcomer Thinks sufficiency means ignoring comfort. Include reasonable comfort and ask what weight it deserves.

When possessions or money are private. Keep discussion with the invented owner. Members may pass or offer a made-up example. Avoid comparisons of income, possessions, or personal sacrifice.

Questions you may be asked

“Can I enjoy having a better thing?” Yes. A preference for quality or enjoyment can be reasonable. Ask what gives it its place among the needs and responsibilities the choice should serve.

“Does gratitude make a benefit a moral good?” The benefit can be useful and welcome. The grateful use of choice is where moral goodness can lie. Appreciating one need not make it necessary for living well.

“Does Stoic joy mean feeling happy about everything?” The lesson connects joy with present moral good, such as honorable purpose and conduct. Gratitude can be quiet or mixed with disappointment. It need not manufacture a mood.

“Must I try living more simply?” The meeting examines the measure through a shared case. The weekly course uses a simpler arrangement to test a perceived need. The point of such a trial is what it reveals about sufficiency and value.

“What if the measure was too low?” Revise it for the actual need. A fair measure includes the work the thing must perform and the people whose welfare depends on it.

Adapting the practice and discussion

  • Mostly newcomers. Stay with the phone’s uses and one extra promise. Keep virtue concrete through wise and fair use.
  • Mostly experienced members. Use the passing offer and battery reveal to test whether a measure remains answerable to evidence.
  • Online. Read the purpose and terms aloud. Invite a short measure from one person and a question about it from another.
  • A large group. Give pairs the same case and hear the differences in the needs their measures preserve.
  • Short on time. Skip the reveal or shorten question 4. Keep the final step that directs desire toward virtue.

After the meeting, send a short recap through your group’s usual channel with one or two insights or open questions and the single take-home invitation. Experiences and fresh questions are equally welcome at the next meeting.
